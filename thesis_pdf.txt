                                      Abstract


Precision, hardware, and backend choices are examined for a stand-alone CPU/CUDA Euler
HRSC code using a second-order finite-volume MUSCL–Hancock scheme with the HLLC
Riemann solver. The validation suite covers Sod, Toro3, Toro5, Liska-Wendroff configuration
3 (LW3), and Liska-Wendroff configuration 12 (LW12), spanning one- and two-dimensional
ideal-gas Euler tests with exact or higher-resolution references; LW12 is compared with an
8002 fp64 numerical reference. The experiments compare real fp32/fp64 builds, matched
strict-HLLC CPU/GPU saved outputs, selected compiler, branch-rule and solver variations,
and Verificarlo/MCA diagnostics, where p32 is treated as virtual precision rather than IEEE
fp32. Across the matched CPU/GPU saved-output set, final conservative states give L1 = 0,
L∞ = 0, and ULPmax = 0, with checkpoint equality limited to saved states. Direct fp32/fp64
gaps remain below the available reference scale in the tested cases; for LW12 at 4002 , the
density reference-scaled ratio is 1.30 × 10−4 . Selected implementation checks show O2/O3
bit-identity where tested, while fast-math perturbs non-stationary saved states. The result
is a bounded Euler baseline for the later MHD precision and hardware study, not MHD
validation.

Introduction

1.1     Context: HRSC Schemes for Discontinuous Compress-
        ible Flows
Compressible-flow simulations are needed when laboratory measurements or exact solutions
cannot describe the full evolution of a flow field. In high-speed gas dynamics and aerospace
or engineering applications, the solution may contain shocks, contact discontinuities, and
rarefactions, so the numerical method must capture sharp waves without replacing them by
non-physical oscillations. At the same time, the calculation has to be accurate enough to
compare with experiments, exact Riemann solutions, or established benchmark references,
while still running in feasible time on available hardware. The longer project also points
towards plasma and MHD problems, but the Report 1 evidence is deliberately restricted
to Euler validation. Finite-volume high-resolution shock-capturing methods provide the
appropriate setting because they evolve conserved cell averages and use Riemann-solver
fluxes to handle discontinuous wave interactions [17]. The same features that make HRSC
methods useful also make their precision and backend reproducibility worth measuring.


1.2     Precision and Hardware Reproducibility Problem
For such schemes, reproducibility is not guaranteed by the statement of the finite-volume
algorithm alone. Two runs may use the same reconstruction, Riemann solver, and time
step, but differ because binary32 and binary64 round quantities differently, because the
compiler fuses operations or reassociates expressions, or because device arithmetic and
parallel ordering change the sequence of rounded operations [7, 11]. These mechanisms
matter in CFD because shock-capturing updates contain branch decisions, limiters, and flux
2                                                                              Introduction


differences near discontinuities, where small arithmetic changes may be amplified rather
than remaining isolated roundoff. Recent CFD studies therefore treat reduced or mixed
precision as case-dependent: Brogi et al. report OpenFOAM evidence across CFD workloads,
while Wang, Xia, and Chen study a heterogeneous hybrid-precision finite-volume method for
compressible flow, set against the wider linear-algebra mixed-precision literature surveyed
by Higham and Mary [10] [2, 20]. These papers motivate validation on the present solver
and test matrix; they do not establish that fp32 is adequate here. CUDA, the NVIDIA GPU
programming model and backend used in this project, can differ from CPU backends in
fp32/fp64 throughput and arithmetic behaviour and can also vary between GPU devices,
so this report measures those effects rather than assuming reproducibility across hardware.
Verificarlo provides precision diagnostics only: direct fp32 claims use real fp32/fp64 runs;
virtual-precision modes should not be treated as IEEE binary32 executions.


1.3     Report Scope and Contribution
Report 1 measures how precision and hardware/backend choices affect a Riemann-solver-
based HRSC method on a controlled Euler validation suite. The evidence base is Euler
ideal-gas validation in one and two space dimensions; MHD motivates the longer project
and the direction of Report 2, but no MHD validation is claimed in Report 1. This boundary
matters because the same numerical questions arise for ideal-MHD solvers, but the magnetic-
field constraint and divergence-control choices require separate validation before they can
support conclusions. Within the tested Euler cases in this report, the report contributes
selected validation evidence for the stand-alone HRSC solver, quantified fp32/fp64 and
matched CPU/GPU saved-output comparisons, and measured sensitivity to selected compiler,
solver, and branch-rule variations. The contribution is therefore a bounded baseline for
the later project rather than a general statement about all HRSC schemes, all hardware, or
untested MHD configurations.


1.4     Report Structure
Chapter 2 reviews the governing background and literature context needed for the Euler
validation study and the later MHD direction. Chapter 3 defines the finite-volume HRSC
method and the Riemann-solver choices used in the experiments. Chapter 4 describes the
implementation and experimental design, including how precision and hardware comparisons
are made comparable. Chapter 5 reports the validation and precision/hardware results.

2.3     HRSC and Benchmark Literature
High-resolution shock-capturing (HRSC) finite-volume methods are a natural choice for
compressible Euler flows because they evolve cell averages in conservative form while allow-
ing discontinuities to be represented without tracking shock or contact positions explicitly.
In Godunov-type methods, the face flux is obtained from a local Riemann problem, so
the approximate Riemann solver is not a secondary implementation detail: it controls the
2.4 Floating-Point Arithmetic and Reproducibility                                                7


dissipation and wave information used at shocks and contact discontinuities [17, 8]. The
Harten–Lax–van Leer (HLL) family gives a standard approximate-flux framework, and the
HLLC extension of Toro et al. [18] restores a contact wave within that family; the algebraic
form used in this report is deferred to Chapter 3.
    Benchmark problems serve a different role from the numerical method itself. They
provide recognisable wave patterns and comparison points against which a solver can be
checked before precision and hardware effects are interpreted. Sod’s shock tube and Toro’s
one-dimensional Riemann problems are standard sources for shocks, contacts, and rarefac-
tions in a controlled setting [15, 17]. Liska and Wendroff’s two-dimensional Euler Riemann
configurations extend this role to interacting waves and multidimensional structure without
requiring the background chapter to catalogue individual test cases [13].


2.4     Floating-Point Arithmetic and Reproducibility
IEEE floating-point arithmetic uses finite significand and exponent fields, so the update is
not evaluated over the real numbers. In round-to-nearest arithmetic, binary32 has a 24-bit
significand and normal exponent range roughly −126 to +127, while binary64 has a 53-bit
significand and range −1022 to +1023 [11, 7]. The corresponding unit roundoff is about
2−24 and 2−53 , respectively [9]; this is a local scale, not a solution-error bound for a nonlinear
time-dependent HRSC calculation.
    The key difficulty is non-associativity. In binary64, (10−18 + 1) − 1 rounds the small
increment away and returns zero, whereas 10−18 + (1 − 1) returns 10−18 . Inside a nonlinear
solver, such changes can alter wave-speed estimates, limiter decisions, fluxes, and later time
steps. The report therefore measures their size and location rather than assuming that they
remain harmless or grow monotonically.
    Compiler and device choices add variation. Ordinary -O3 may change instruction selec-
tion, but fast-math or -Ofast can permit reassociation, FMA contraction, reciprocal-based
division, and approximate square-root paths. Since floating-point addition is non-associative,
GPU block or warp scheduling and atomic reductions can change the accumulation tree;
the solver comparisons below avoid summation-order claims unless an ordered max/min or
saved-state metric supports them. Comparison order can also matter for tied or nearly tied
candidates. CFD precision studies such as Brogi et al.’s OpenFOAM work show why reduced
or mixed precision must be validated case by case [2]. This report also uses Verificarlo,
which implements Monte Carlo arithmetic (MCA) by perturbing floating-point operations to
estimate rounding sensitivity [14, 5]. Its p32 setting is a virtual mantissa precision used for
diagnostics, not IEEE binary32.

2.5     Report 1 Gap
Together, the literature reviewed here separates three tasks. HRSC and Riemann-solver work
provide the method foundations for discontinuous Euler flow; Sod, Toro, and Liska–Wendroff
benchmarks define controlled validation settings; and floating-point studies explain why
arithmetic format, compiler choices, and device execution can alter a nominally identical
computation. The remaining gap for Report 1 is to measure precision and hardware sensitivity
under controlled Euler validation tests, before treating ideal MHD as a later extension.

tion mechanism used below, following the standard treatments of Toro [17] and LeVeque
[12]. The explicit time step is controlled by a Courant–Friedrichs–Lewy (CFL) condition
on local acoustic wave speeds; the full formula is given in Section 3.4. Equation 3.6 fixes
the two-direction notation; the implementation applies this conservative update through
alternating directional sweeps, described in Chapter 4. In two dimensions each sweep is a
one-dimensional conservative update using the current cell averages; alternating the sweep
order reduces a fixed ordering bias. The scheme is therefore dimensionally split, not an
unsplit method with transverse flux corrections.

smooth monotone regions the piecewise-linear data and Hancock predictor give the expected
second-order truncation behaviour. At extrema, shocks, contacts, and rarefaction edges the
minbee/minmod-type limiter can set or reduce the slope, moving the local update towards a
first-order Godunov step [19, 17].

Rounding model. A useful local model for the floating-point part of the method is the
standard one-operation rounding model

                         fl(a op b) = (a op b)(1 + δ ),    |δ | ≤ u,                   (3.26)

precision throughout [11, 9].

3.6     Extension to Ideal MHD
Report 1 uses Euler, the hydrodynamic limit of the later ideal-MHD problem. In ideal MHD
the conserved state augments (ρ, ρu, E) with B, adding magnetic pressure, tension, and
induction terms. Its Riemann fan has seven families: two fast magnetosonic, two Alfven,
two slow magnetosonic, and one entropy/contact wave, making it more complex than Euler’s
three-wave HLLC fan [17]. The magnetic field must satisfy

                                         ∇ · B = 0,                                          (3.30)

at the continuous level, but a finite-volume update does not enforce this constraint automati-
cally; discrete divergence can create spurious magnetic forces and distort wave speeds.
    Dedner et al.’s hyperbolic cleaning approach [4] augments the induction equation with a
scalar field ψ:

               ∂t B + ∇ · (uB − Bu) + ∇ψ = 0,         ∂t ψ + c2h ∇ · B = −ψ/τ.               (3.31)

Here ψ carries divergence error, ch is the cleaning-wave speed, and τ is the damping time.
Constrained transport [6] instead preserves a staggered-mesh discrete divergence constraint.
This is also why the Euler HLLC flux is not a direct full-MHD choice: its three-wave
acoustic/contact model does not resolve the fast, Alfven, slow, and contact families, nor the
18                                                                         Numerical Method


discrete divergence constraint. HLL gives a simpler but more diffusive bound [8]; HLLD and
Roe-type solvers aim to retain more MHD wave structure, while GLM-Dedner cleaning and
constrained transport treat divergence error differently. These are Report 2 concerns. Report 1
evidence remains Euler-only. The added magnetic pressure terms and near-degenerate wave
speeds provide more branch and cancellation sites than Euler, so they require separate
validation.

4.5     Reference-Solution Strategy
The reference solution is part of the experimental design, because a float–double or CPU–
GPU difference is only meaningful once it has a scale. For Sod and the Toro shock tubes used
in the validation tables, the comparison is made against analytic exact Riemann references,
so the reported error mixes discretisation and floating-point effects in a controlled one-
dimensional setting described by Toro [17]. For the two-dimensional Liska–Wendroff
problems, no exact reference is used. LW3 is compared with a 16002 fp64 numerical
reference, and Liska–Wendroff configuration 12 (LW12) is compared with an 8002 fp64
numerical reference in the benchmark family of Liska and Wendroff [13].
    The lower-grid two-dimensional comparisons map these high-resolution references to the
candidate grid before metrics are computed. The conserved variables in the reference grid
are averaged over integer-ratio cell blocks, then the candidate and block-averaged reference
states are converted to primitive variables for the reported norms and SSIM. Thus LW3
maps 16002 to 4002 or 2002 by 4 × 4 or 8 × 8 block averaging, while LW12 maps 8002 to
4002 or 2002 by 2 × 2 or 4 × 4 block averaging. This is a conservative mapping step, not an
exact-solution construction.
    Ratios such as ∥Ufp32 − Ufp64 ∥1 /∥Ufp64 − Uref ∥1 bound precision drift relative to the
reference scale, not absolute terms [9]. CPU/GPU entries are matched same-precision
final-state comparisons; checkpointed summaries bound only saved-output behaviour, not
stage-by-stage identity.

5.1     Validation Overview
Chapter 5 reports the validation and precision evidence generated from the experimental
design in Chapter 4. The design matrix, metric definitions, and reference choices are fixed in
Table 4.2; the role of this chapter is to interpret the measured outputs rather than to redefine
the test suite. The results answer four questions: whether the MUSCL–Hancock–HLLC
implementation reproduces the selected Euler benchmarks; how real fp32 and fp64 outputs
differ when scaled against reference or discretisation error; whether matched strict CPU/GPU
runs reproduce saved conservative states; and how selected compiler, branch-rule, solver, and
time-resolved variation axes affect the final or checkpointed outputs.
    The chapter starts with one-dimensional Riemann tests, then moves to the two Liska-
Wendroff two-dimensional configurations. Precision comparisons are reported mainly as
pairwise fp32–fp64 differences or reference-scaled ratios; separate exact errors appear
only for the one-dimensional cases with exact Riemann references. CPU/GPU entries are
likewise bounded: they compare matched strict-HLLC saved outputs for the listed cases
and precisions. This separation keeps the result claims tied to their evidence and leaves the
broader interpretation of precision and hardware sensitivity to the discussion chapter.

The two-dimensional tests extend the validation from isolated shock-tube waves to interacting
Riemann structures. The selected cases are configurations 3 and 12 from Liska and Wendroff

Discussion

6.1     Validation as the basis for interpretation
Validation makes the later precision and hardware discussion interpretable, because the
numerical differences are scaled before they are judged. Exact Riemann and high-resolution
numerical references give fp32/fp64 differences a meaningful denominator through Equa-
tion 4.2, so a pairwise norm is read against reference or discretisation error rather than in
isolation. Matched within-case binaries make the CPU/GPU comparison a strict saved-output
equality test, not a loose backend comparison. Compiler, branch-rule, solver, fp32 compiler,
and finite-time drift axes are then read alongside precision and device axes, so implementation
sensitivity is compared on the same case set. Sections 6.2–6.4 therefore read the evidence
axis-by-axis, not case-by-case.


6.2     Precision Adequacy and Region-Aware Diagnostics
The direct IEEE fp32/fp64 comparison is most useful when read through the reference-
scaled ratio of Chapter 4 Equation (4.2). For the non-degenerate reference comparisons in
the tested Euler set, the pairwise final-state differences stay below the available reference
or discretisation scale, but the largest reported density ratio, Rρ ≈ 1.30 × 10−4 for Liska-
Wendroff configuration 12 (LW12) at 4002 , is still informative. It occurs with a localised
upper-right shock-interaction difference in Fig. 5.7. That localisation suggests sensitivity
where reconstructed states feed HLLC wave-speed or flux decisions, but it does not prove
that a specific HLLC branch changed. The stationary-contact infinite ratio is a degenerate-
reference case, because its denominator is zero, not evidence of fp32 failure. These ratios
42                                                                                 Discussion


also compress spatial structure into a norm, so the following diagnostics are used to interpret
where precision pressure concentrates rather than to introduce a separate IEEE-fp32 claim.
    Parker’s Monte Carlo Arithmetic perturbs floating-point operations to form an ensemble,
and Denis et al.’s Verificarlo applies that idea to compiled programs [14, 5]. In this report,
the MCA ensemble gives the per-cell distribution needed to locate sensitivity rather than only
estimate a single global rounding scale. The Sod overlay in Fig. 6.1 shows how the ensemble
spread and significant-digit estimates are read from a one-dimensional diagnostic before the
same idea is used on LW3. Figure 6.2 then uses the σFP local roundoff scale of Chapter 4
Section 4.3: for both HLLC and Rusanov, σFP falls as virtual mantissa precision rises. Lower
or similar Rusanov values are read as the combined effect of a different arithmetic path and
extra diffusion smoothing gradients, not as an accuracy ranking or as reproducibility drift.
The virtual labels p8, p16, p32, and p53 are diagnostic mantissa settings, not IEEE binary
formats; Verificarlo virtual precision p32 must therefore not be read as IEEE fp32.

6.4     Limitations and Report 2 Direction
The limitations are evidence-specific. LW12 uses an 8002 numerical reference, not an exact
solution, and LW3 relies on high-resolution self-convergence. Compiler evidence uses one
compiler family inside each case, with the cross-case toolchain split already noted above.
Verificarlo p32 is virtual precision, and the region-margin diagnostics use small MCA sample
counts. Toro2 with the strict < HLLC branch did not complete inside 600 s, whereas the ≤
branch completed; this is consistent with time-step shrinkage near zero wave speeds, but the
claim is limited to observed non-completion. Limiter selection remains conceptual rather
than measured. The validated scope is ideal-gas Euler only: matched CPU/GPU evidence
covers five selected cases, and no MHD validation is claimed. Report 2 should extend
the framework to ideal-MHD tests with divergence control, with Dedner-type cleaning or
constrained transport treated as candidate numerical choices.
Chapter 7

Conclusion

7.1     Aim and Evidence Base
The central question was how precision and hardware/backend choices affect a Riemann-
solver-based HRSC method on a controlled Euler validation suite. Evidence came from one-
and two-dimensional Euler cases, matched CPU/GPU saved-output comparisons, compiler
and branch-rule variations, and region-aware precision diagnostics.


7.2     Key Findings
Matched strict-HLLC CPU/GPU saved outputs were bit-identical within each tested case on
its Windows BuildTools or Linux/WSL toolchain, with zero L1 , L∞ , and ULPmax ; checkpoint
equality applies only to saved states. Implementation sensitivity was axis-dependent: O2/O3
comparisons were bit-identical where tested, fast-math changed non-stationary saved states,
and HLLC–Rusanov was deliberate method variation. Direct fp32/fp64 gaps stayed below
the available reference scale; for LW12 at 4002 , the density ratio was 1.30 × 10−4 , while
Verificarlo p32, a virtual rather than IEEE fp32 mode, located remaining precision pressure
on LW3 fronts. Together these findings provide a bounded Euler baseline for the later
precision and hardware study.


7.3     Limitation and Next Step
The baseline does not cover untested compilers, devices, limiter choices, internal time-step
stages, or MHD. Report 2 should add an Orszag–Tang MHD validation entry with Dedner-
