\chapter{Validation and Precision Results}

\section{Validation Matrix}
% <<SECTION_1_BEGIN>>
Chapter~5 treats validation as a controlled study rather than as a sequence of
plots. It uses the MUSCL--Hancock--HLLC algorithm described in Chapter~3 and
the comparability rules from Chapter~4. The matrix in
Table~\ref{tab:validation-matrix} fixes the cases used to
answer four questions: whether the MUSCL--Hancock--HLLC solver reproduces
standard Euler benchmarks; how real fp32 and fp64 runs differ; whether matched
CPU and GPU executions reproduce the same conservative state; and how sensitive
the conclusions are to selected implementation and compiler variations. Sod is
included as the exact-reference shock tube. The Toro cases add one-dimensional
strong-wave tests, including the right-running supersonic shock in Toro3 and the
collision of supersonic shocks in Toro5. The two-dimensional set uses
Liska-Wendroff configuration 3 (LW3) and Liska-Wendroff configuration 12
(LW12), both of which contain interacting supersonic quadrant-interface shocks.

The reported norms are computed on the conservative state unless a scalar field
is named. $L_1$ denotes the mean absolute difference over cells and conserved
variables, while $L_\infty$ is the maximum absolute cellwise difference. ULP
counts measure the largest representable-spacing difference between paired
floating-point outputs. SSIM is used only for two-dimensional image-like
comparisons against a higher-resolution reference. Reference-scaled ratios divide
the fp32--fp64 difference by the fp64--reference error; discretisation-scaled
ratios use the same idea when the denominator is a grid-refinement error rather
than an exact solution. Thus a ratio below one means that the precision gap is
smaller than the measured reference or discretisation error, not that fp32 is
adequate in general.

\section{One-Dimensional Euler Validation}
% <<SECTION_2_BEGIN>>
The one-dimensional tests are placed before the precision and hardware
interpretation because they establish the baseline question: whether the
implemented Euler solver captures the expected Riemann structure of the
MUSCL--Hancock--HLLC path~\citep{toro2009} before small
fp32--fp64 or CPU--GPU differences are assigned any meaning. Sod provides the
least severe check, with a left rarefaction, contact discontinuity, and
right-moving shock from the standard shock-tube problem~\citep{sod_1978}. In
Fig.~\ref{fig:ch5-sod-comparison}, the computed density, velocity, and pressure
profiles follow the exact solution across all three waves. The remaining
discrepancy is concentrated at discontinuities, as expected for a finite-volume
shock-capturing method~\citep{leveque_2002}, rather than appearing as a
systematic offset in the smooth parts of the solution.

Toro3 and Toro5 then stress the same code path with stronger waves from the
Toro test set~\citep{toro2009}. Toro3 has a right-running supersonic shock, and
Fig.~\ref{fig:ch5-toro3-comparison} shows that the shock, contact, and
rarefaction are placed on the same branches as the exact solution. Toro5
involves collision of supersonic shocks; Fig.~\ref{fig:ch5-toro5-comparison}
therefore checks a more severe pressure and velocity interaction than Sod. The
profiles do not remove the usual grid-scale smearing at sharp waves, but they
show that the solver resolves the ordering and amplitude of the main
one-dimensional structures used later in the validation matrix.

The numerical summaries support the same interpretation. At $N=200$, the
final-state fp64--fp32 $L_1$ difference on the conservative state is
$8.743340\times10^{-8}$ for Sod, $6.386967\times10^{-5}$ for Toro3, and
$1.296410\times10^{-4}$ for Toro5. These are pairwise precision differences,
not separate exact-solution errors for either precision. Against the
one-dimensional exact-reference regression at $N=800$, the Toro3 density
fp32--fp64 $L_1$ gap is $1.364064\times10^{-5}$ of the fp64--exact
$L_1$ error, while the corresponding Toro5 $R_\rho^{\mathrm{exact}}$ is
$3.913105\times10^{-5}$. Thus the measured precision gap is well below the
reference error scale for these strong one-dimensional tests. The matched
CPU/GPU HLLC comparisons give $L_1=0$, $L_\infty=0$, and $\mathrm{ULP}_{\max}=0$
for both Toro3 and Toro5 in fp64 and fp32 at final time, so the later hardware
discussion starts from bit-identical one-dimensional final states for these
cases.

\section{Two-Dimensional Euler Validation}
% <<SECTION_3_BEGIN>>
The two-dimensional tests extend the validation from isolated shock-tube waves
to interacting Riemann structures. Liska-Wendroff configuration 3 (LW3) is the
four-shock case from the Liska--Wendroff benchmark set, with supersonic shock
segments initially lying on the left, right, upper, and lower quadrant
interfaces around the central cross~\citep{liska_wendroff_2003}. In the
$N=400$ fp64 schlieren field, Fig.~\ref{fig:ch5-lw3-schlieren} shows these
segments evolving into a compact interaction region: a diamond-like set of
shock fronts surrounds the centre, while curved contact-like structures and a
small rolled-up feature remain inside the shocked region. The original
axis-aligned discontinuities are still visible away from the interaction zone,
which is expected at this finite time because the outer waves have not crossed
the whole domain. The quantitative comparison is consistent with the visual
interpretation. Against the $1600^2$ numerical reference, the fp64 density
$L_1$ error decreases from $7.89\times10^{-3}$ at $N=200$ to
$4.95\times10^{-3}$ at $N=400$, while density SSIM increases from 0.966 to
0.982. The same-resolution fp64--fp32 conservative-state $L_1$ difference at
$N=400$ is $3.24\times10^{-7}$, much smaller than the reference-scale
density error.

Liska-Wendroff configuration 12 (LW12) is treated as a separate two-dimensional
Riemann problem, not as a repeat of LW3. Its initial data contain two
supersonic shock segments, one on the upper vertical quadrant interface and one
on the right horizontal quadrant interface, together with two contact
interfaces~\citep{liska_wendroff_2003}. Figure~\ref{fig:ch5-lw12-schlieren}
has a different topology from LW3: the $N=400$ fp64 schlieren image contains a
large curved shock arc reaching towards the upper-right corner, a second curved
front below it, and a small shear/contact roll-up near the central interaction.
This separates LW12 from LW3 as a second multidimensional validation case. Its
reference comparison uses a higher-resolution $800^2$ fp64 numerical
reference, not an exact solution. With that reference, the fp64 density $L_1$
error decreases from $2.95\times10^{-3}$ at $N=200$ to
$1.33\times10^{-3}$ at $N=400$, and density SSIM increases from about 0.989
to about 0.996. The fp32--fp64 density difference remains reference-scaled at
$1.30\times10^{-4}$ for $N=400$, so the visual agreement is backed by a
measured error scale.

\section{Single- and Double-Precision Comparison}
% <<SECTION_4_BEGIN>>
The fp32--fp64 comparison is most useful when it is scaled against the error
already present in the discretisation or reference comparison. For a scalar
field $\phi$, the reported direct-precision ratio is
\begin{equation*}
R_\phi(N)=
\frac{\|\phi_{\mathrm{fp32}}(N)-\phi_{\mathrm{fp64}}(N)\|_1}
     {\|\phi_{\mathrm{fp64}}(N)-\phi_{\mathrm{ref}}\|_1}.
\end{equation*}
Figure~\ref{fig:ch5-float-double-over-reference} reports $R_\phi$ for the
two-dimensional LW3 case. For density, $R_\rho=4.47\times10^{-5}$ at
$N=200$ and $R_\rho=9.25\times10^{-5}$ at $N=400$. The ratio therefore
remains far below one, but it grows with resolution because the reference error
shrinks faster than the fp32--fp64 drift. The same pattern appears in the
second two-dimensional case: for LW12 against the $800^2$ fp64 reference,
$R_\rho=4.63\times10^{-5}$ at $N=200$ and
$R_\rho=1.30\times10^{-4}$ at $N=400$.

The one-dimensional exact-reference rows give a similar scale for the selected
shock-tube tests. At $N=800$, the density fp32--fp64 gap divided by the
fp64--exact error is $1.06\times10^{-4}$ for Sod,
$1.36\times10^{-5}$ for Toro3, and $3.91\times10^{-5}$ for Toro5.
The stationary-contact case is reported separately because its exact-stationary pressure makes the denominator zero. In particular, the
stationary-contact pressure row has $L_1$ fp32--fp64 divided by fp64--exact
reported as $\infty$, because the fp64 pressure error against the exact
stationary state is zero. This row is therefore a degenerate-reference result,
not a quantitative precision-adequacy ratio.

These measurements show that, for the tested Euler cases, the final-time
fp32--fp64 differences are smaller than the available reference or
discretisation error estimates~\citep{higham_2002}. They do not show that fp32 is generally
sufficient: the ratios are case-, field-, resolution-, and final-time metrics,
and the increasing two-dimensional density ratios show that the comparison can
move as the numerical reference improves. Virtual-precision sensitivity maps
are discussed separately in Chapter~6 rather than used as direct fp32 evidence
here.

\section{Matched CPU/GPU Comparison}
% <<SECTION_5_BEGIN>>

study: for a fixed case, precision, solver, and binary, does changing the
runtime device alter the final conservative state? Table~\ref{tab:ch5-cpu-gpu}
summarises the available strict-HLLC evidence. All rows use \texttt{solver=hllc}

comparison is byte-level for the saved final \texttt{grid.bin} files, with the
reported $L_1$, $L_\infty$, and ULP metrics computed on the conservative
state. Byte identity therefore implies all three drift measures are zero, but

\section{Compiler, Branch, Solver, and Drift-Growth Sensitivity}
% <<SECTION_6_BEGIN>>
\subsection*{Compiler, branch-rule, and solver variation}

Table~\ref{tab:ch5-variation} separates three sensitivity axes which should
not be interpreted in the same way. The first table uses CPU fp64 final states.
Branch-rule and compiler-flag rows cover the selected one-dimensional
strong-wave cases as well as Sod, stationary contact, and LW3-N200; the
solver-variation rows are limited to the three cases for which a Rusanov
comparison was run. The branch-rule comparison changes only the HLLC
wave-speed test controlled by \texttt{RIEMANN\_STRICT\_INEQUALITY}: the
baseline uses $\leq$, while the strict-inequality build uses $<$. The
compiler-flag comparison keeps HLLC fixed and compares O2, O3, and
Ofast with fast-math. The solver-variation comparison is different: HLLC and
Rusanov are deliberately different numerical fluxes, so the reported difference
is a method effect rather than reproducibility drift.

ranges are therefore screening metrics, not convergence studies or estimates of

\subsection*{Time-resolved drift and Toro2 branch stability}

Figure~\ref{fig:ch5-drift-timeseries} uses ten synchronized checkpoints per
case, from 10\% to 100\% of each run time, to show how $L_1$ differences
develop under the same O2 versus Ofast+fast-math comparison. The fitted
$\lambda_{\mathrm{fit}}$ values are finite-time slopes of $\log(L_1)$
against solver time over those ten samples,
\begin{equation*}
\log L_1(t_k)=a+\lambda_{\mathrm{fit}}t_k .
\end{equation*}
This slope is a finite-time Lyapunov-like growth rate in the
implementation-drift sense, in the spirit of the time-series fit used
by~\citet{wolf_etal_1985} and the broader chaotic-attractor framing
of~\citet{eckmann_ruelle_1985}; it diagnoses how quickly compiler-level
rounding differences amplify in the computed solution and is not a continuous
PDE-level dynamical-system exponent. Table~\ref{tab:ch5-lyapunov} reports the slopes
for the strict-IEEE O2 versus Ofast+fast-math implementation pair across all
six one-dimensional cases. Toro3 has the largest density and pressure slopes,
297.7 and 254.8; Toro5 density (111.1) and Toro4 pressure (96.79) are next,
followed by Toro5 pressure (94.56) and Toro4 density (86.38); the
stationary-contact control remains exactly zero.
