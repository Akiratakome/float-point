\chapter{Validation and Precision Results}

\section{Validation Overview}
% <<SECTION_1_BEGIN>>
Chapter~5 reports the validation and precision evidence generated from the
experimental design in Chapter~4. The design matrix, metric definitions, and
reference choices are fixed in Table~\ref{tab:ch4-design-matrix}; the role of
this chapter is to interpret the measured outputs rather than to redefine the
test suite. The results answer four questions: whether the
MUSCL--Hancock--HLLC implementation reproduces the selected Euler benchmarks;
how real fp32 and fp64 outputs differ when scaled against reference or
discretisation error; whether matched strict CPU/GPU runs reproduce saved
conservative states; and how selected compiler, branch-rule, solver, and
time-resolved variation axes affect the final or checkpointed outputs.

The chapter starts with one-dimensional Riemann tests, then moves to the two
Liska-Wendroff two-dimensional configurations. Precision comparisons are
reported only as pairwise fp32--fp64 differences or reference-scaled ratios,
not as separate fp32 or fp64 exact errors. CPU/GPU entries are likewise bounded:
they compare matched strict-HLLC saved outputs for the listed cases and
precisions. This separation keeps the result claims tied to their evidence and
leaves the broader interpretation of precision and hardware sensitivity to the
discussion chapter.
% <<SECTION_1_END>>

\section{One-Dimensional Euler Validation}
% <<SECTION_2_BEGIN>>
The one-dimensional tests are placed before the precision and hardware
interpretation because they establish the baseline question: whether the
implemented Euler solver captures the expected Riemann structure of the
MUSCL--Hancock--HLLC path described by \citet{toro2009} before small
fp32--fp64 or CPU--GPU differences are assigned any meaning. Sod provides the
least severe check, with a left rarefaction, contact discontinuity, and
right-moving shock from the standard shock-tube problem of \citet{sod_1978}. In
Fig.~\ref{fig:ch5-sod-comparison}, the computed density, velocity, and pressure
profiles follow the exact solution across all three waves. The remaining
discrepancy is concentrated at discontinuities, as expected for a finite-volume
shock-capturing method~\citep{leveque_2002}, rather than appearing as a
systematic offset in the smooth parts of the solution.

Toro3 and Toro5 then stress the same code path with stronger waves from the
test set of \citet{toro2009}. Toro3 has a right-running supersonic shock, and
Fig.~\ref{fig:ch5-toro3-comparison} shows that the shock, contact, and
rarefaction are placed on the same branches as the exact solution. Toro5
involves collision of supersonic shocks; Fig.~\ref{fig:ch5-toro5-comparison}
therefore checks a more severe pressure and velocity interaction than Sod. The
profiles do not remove the usual grid-scale smearing at sharp waves, but they
show that the solver resolves the ordering and amplitude of the main
one-dimensional structures fixed in the Chapter~4 design matrix.

The numerical summaries support the same interpretation. At $N=200$, the
final-state fp64--fp32 $L_1$ difference on the conservative state is
$8.743340\times10^{-8}$ for Sod, $6.386967\times10^{-5}$ for Toro3, and
$1.296410\times10^{-4}$ for Toro5. These are pairwise precision differences,
not separate exact-solution errors for either precision. Against the
one-dimensional exact-reference regression at $N=800$, the Toro3 density
fp32--fp64 $L_1$ gap is $1.364064\times10^{-5}$ of the fp64--exact
$L_1$ error, while the corresponding Toro5 $R_\rho^{\mathrm{exact}}$ is
$3.913105\times10^{-5}$. Thus the measured precision gap is well below the
reference error scale for these strong one-dimensional tests. The matched
CPU/GPU HLLC comparisons give $L_1=0$, $L_\infty=0$, and $\mathrm{ULP}_{\max}=0$
for both Toro3 and Toro5 in fp64 and fp32 at final time, so the later hardware
discussion starts from bit-identical one-dimensional final states for these
cases.

\section{Two-Dimensional Euler Validation}
% <<SECTION_3_BEGIN>>
The two-dimensional tests extend the validation from isolated shock-tube waves
to interacting Riemann structures. The selected cases are configurations 3 and
12 from \citet{liska_wendroff_2003}. Liska-Wendroff
configuration 3 (LW3) is the four-shock case from that benchmark set, with
supersonic shock segments initially lying on the left, right, upper, and lower
quadrant interfaces around the central cross. In the
$N=400$ fp64 schlieren field, Fig.~\ref{fig:ch5-lw3-schlieren} shows these
segments evolving into a compact interaction region: a diamond-like set of
shock fronts surrounds the centre, while curved contact-like structures and a
small rolled-up feature remain inside the shocked region. The original
axis-aligned discontinuities are still visible away from the interaction zone,
which is expected at this finite time because the outer waves have not crossed
the whole domain. The quantitative comparison is consistent with the visual
interpretation. Against the $1600^2$ numerical reference, the fp64 density
$L_1$ error decreases from $7.89\times10^{-3}$ at $N=200$ to
$4.95\times10^{-3}$ at $N=400$, while density SSIM increases from 0.966 to
0.982. The same-resolution fp64--fp32 conservative-state $L_1$ difference at
$N=400$ is $3.24\times10^{-7}$, much smaller than the reference-scale
density error.

Liska-Wendroff configuration 12 (LW12) is treated as a separate
two-dimensional Riemann problem, not as a repeat of LW3. Its initial data contain two
supersonic shock segments, one on the upper vertical quadrant interface and one
on the right horizontal quadrant interface, together with two contact
interfaces, again following \citet{liska_wendroff_2003}.
Figure~\ref{fig:ch5-lw12-schlieren}
has a different topology from LW3: the $N=400$ fp64 schlieren image contains a
large curved shock arc reaching towards the upper-right corner, a second curved
front below it, and a small shear/contact roll-up near the central interaction.
This separates LW12 from LW3 as a second multidimensional validation case. Its
reference comparison uses a higher-resolution $800^2$ fp64 numerical
reference, not an exact solution. With that reference, the fp64 density $L_1$
error decreases from $2.95\times10^{-3}$ at $N=200$ to
$1.33\times10^{-3}$ at $N=400$, and density SSIM increases from about 0.989
to about 0.996. The fp32--fp64 density difference remains reference-scaled at
$1.30\times10^{-4}$ for $N=400$, so the visual agreement is backed by a
measured error scale.

\section{Single- and Double-Precision Comparison}
% <<SECTION_4_BEGIN>>
The direct IEEE fp32--fp64 comparison is most useful when it is scaled against the error
already present in the discretisation or reference comparison. For a scalar
field $\phi$, the reported direct IEEE precision ratio is
\begin{equation*}
R_\phi(N)=
\frac{\|\phi_{\mathrm{fp32}}(N)-\phi_{\mathrm{fp64}}(N)\|_1}
     {\|\phi_{\mathrm{fp64}}(N)-\phi_{\mathrm{ref}}\|_1}.
\end{equation*}
Figure~\ref{fig:ch5-float-double-over-reference} reports $R_\phi$ for the
two-dimensional LW3 case. For density, $R_\rho=4.47\times10^{-5}$ at
$N=200$ and $R_\rho=9.25\times10^{-5}$ at $N=400$. The ratio therefore
remains far below one, but it grows with resolution because the reference error
shrinks faster than the fp32--fp64 drift. The same pattern appears in the
second two-dimensional case: for LW12 against the $800^2$ fp64 reference,
$R_\rho=4.63\times10^{-5}$ at $N=200$ and
$R_\rho=1.30\times10^{-4}$ at $N=400$.

The one-dimensional exact-reference comparisons give a similar scale for the selected
shock-tube tests. At $N=800$, the density fp32--fp64 gap divided by the
fp64--exact error is $1.06\times10^{-4}$ for Sod,
$1.36\times10^{-5}$ for Toro3, and $3.91\times10^{-5}$ for Toro5.
The stationary-contact case is reported separately because its exact-stationary
pressure makes the denominator zero. In particular, the stationary-contact
pressure comparison has $L_1$ fp32--fp64 divided by fp64--exact
reported as $\infty$, because the fp64 pressure error against the exact
stationary state is zero. This case is therefore a degenerate-reference result,
not a quantitative precision-adequacy ratio.

These measurements show that, for the tested Euler cases, the final-time direct
fp32--fp64 differences are smaller than the available reference or
discretisation error estimates~\citep{higham_2002}. They do not show that fp32
is generally sufficient: the ratios are case-, field-, resolution-, and final-time metrics,
and the increasing two-dimensional density ratios show that the comparison can
move as the numerical reference improves. The localised LW12 density difference
in Fig.~\ref{fig:ch5-lw12-precision-heatmap} is consistent with precision
sensitivity where reconstructed left and right states feed the HLLC wave-speed
estimates and flux decisions near the upper-right shock interaction. It does
not, by itself, prove that a specific HLLC branch changed; no branch-count diagnostic
is used for that figure. Virtual-precision sensitivity maps
are discussed separately in Chapter~6 rather than used as direct fp32 evidence
here.

\section{Compiler, Branch, Solver, and Drift-Growth Sensitivity}
% <<SECTION_6_BEGIN>>
\subsection*{Compiler, branch-rule, and solver variation}

Table~\ref{tab:ch5-variation} separates the sensitivity axes which should
not be interpreted in the same way. It reports representative verified
comparisons rather than cross-case ranges. The branch-rule comparison changes
only the HLLC wave-speed test controlled by the branch-rule macro defined in
Chapter~3: the baseline uses $\leq$, while the strict-inequality build uses
$<$. The compiler comparison keeps HLLC fixed.
The solver comparison is different: HLLC and Rusanov are deliberately
different numerical fluxes, so the reported difference is a method effect
rather than reproducibility drift. The compiler switches themselves are listed
in Chapter~4; here they are used only as named experimental axes.

A GPU flag probe tests the build-control side of the matched CPU/GPU result.
The fast CUDA build disables the strict-IEEE option and enables fast-math, so
the host path uses fast-math optimisation and nvcc reverts to its default
fused-operation and denormal-handling choices. The per-kernel FMA pin remains
unchanged. Fast CUDA outputs are compared against strict CUDA outputs at the
same case, precision, and grid.
Sod at $N=200$ in fp64 acquires $L_1=2.89\times10^{-17}$,
$L_\infty=3.66\times10^{-15}$, $\mathrm{ULP}_{\max}\approx6.6$; LW3 at
$200^2$ cells in fp64 grows to $\mathrm{ULP}_{\max}\approx23.3$; the matching
LW3 fp32 comparison reaches
$L_1=3.62\times10^{-7}$, $L_\infty=1.57\times10^{-5}$,
$\mathrm{ULP}_{\max}\approx30.7$. Sod at $N=200$ in fp32 happens to remain
bit-identical. This supplementary GPU flag probe does not constitute a
non-strict CPU/GPU comparison, because both sides are CUDA outputs. The probe
is used only to show that
the strict build flags can materially affect the saved GPU state, so the
zero-drift CPU/GPU statement in Table~\ref{tab:ch5-cpu-gpu} is bounded to the
strict-IEEE, fast-math-off configuration rather than to CUDA execution in
general.

Limiter sensitivity is not reported as a result in this chapter. The available
experiment summary records that the report experiment setup has no documented
limiter-selection axis, so producing such a result during writing would require a
new opt-in interface or a solver-numerics change.

\subsection*{Time-resolved drift and Toro2 branch stability}

Figure~\ref{fig:ch5-drift-timeseries} uses ten synchronized checkpoints per
case, from 10\% to 100\% of each case duration, to show how $L_1$ differences
develop under the compiler and branch-rule comparisons. The main-text point is
the checkpoint ordering rather than a fitted-rate table: the largest visible
absolute drifts are the pressure curves for Toro5 and Toro3, followed by the
Toro5 density curve, while Sod, Toro2, Toro4, and the stationary-contact
control remain in lower or zero-drift bands. The comparison is finite-time and
checkpoint-based; it is not interpreted as a continuous dynamical-system rate.
