\chapter{Validation and Precision Results}

\section{Validation Overview}
% <<SECTION_1_BEGIN>>
Chapter~5 interprets the Chapter~4 design matrix outputs: one- and
two-dimensional Euler validation, direct fp32--fp64 differences, matched
strict-HLLC CPU/GPU saved-output comparisons, and selected method or toolchain
sensitivity probes. Precision claims are pairwise differences or
reference-scaled ratios; CPU/GPU claims are restricted to saved conservative
states.
% <<SECTION_1_END>>

\section{One-Dimensional Euler Validation}
% <<SECTION_2_BEGIN>>
The one-dimensional tests first check whether the MUSCL--Hancock--HLLC path
captures the expected Riemann structures of \citet{toro2009} before smaller
fp32--fp64 or CPU--GPU differences are interpreted. Sod provides the standard
rarefaction--contact--shock check of \citet{sod_1978}; in
Fig.~\ref{fig:ch5-sod-comparison}, density, velocity, and pressure follow the
exact solution, with the feature-scale comparison quantified in
Table~\ref{tab:ch5-1d-feature-validation}.

Toro3 and Toro5 stress the same code path with stronger waves. Toro3 contains a
right-running supersonic shock, while Toro5 involves colliding supersonic
shocks. Figures~\ref{fig:ch5-toro3-comparison}
and~\ref{fig:ch5-toro5-comparison} show the expected wave ordering with
grid-scale smearing at sharp waves.

The numerical summaries support the same interpretation. At $N=200$, the
conservative-state fp64--fp32 $L_1$ difference spans $\sim 10^{-8}$ to
$\sim 10^{-4}$ across Sod, Toro3, and Toro5; the corresponding
reference-scaled density ratios at $N=800$ remain below $10^{-4}$
(Table~\ref{tab:ch5-1d-summary}). These are pairwise precision differences,
not separate exact-solution errors. The matched
CPU/GPU HLLC comparisons give $L_1=0$, $L_\infty=0$, and $\mathrm{ULP}_{\max}=0$
for both Toro3 and Toro5 in fp64 and fp32 at final time, so the later hardware
discussion starts from zero-drift saved one-dimensional final states for these
matched cases.

Table~\ref{tab:ch5-1d-exact-convergence} gives the exact density errors behind
this scale. The observed orders are below two, as expected for shock-tube
solutions whose global $L_1$ error is dominated by discontinuity neighbourhoods.
In smooth regions MUSCL--Hancock can recover higher-order behaviour, but a shock
or contact leaves an $O(1)$ jump smeared over $O(\Delta x)$ cells, so the
domain-integrated error retains a first-order component. The band split in
Table~\ref{tab:ch5-1d-feature-validation} is consistent with this: narrow
discontinuity bands contribute much larger density error than the remaining
cells.

\section{Two-Dimensional Euler Validation}
% <<SECTION_3_BEGIN>>
The two-dimensional tests extend the validation from isolated shock tubes to
interacting Riemann structures. Liska-Wendroff configuration 3 (LW3) is the
four-shock case from \citet{liska_wendroff_2003}, with supersonic shock
segments on all four quadrant interfaces. In the $N=400$ fp64 schlieren field,
Fig.~\ref{fig:ch5-lw3-schlieren} shows a compact diamond-like shock interaction
around the centre, with curved contact-like structures inside the shocked
region. The reference comparisons use report-specific higher-resolution fp64
runs, not exact or literature-converged solutions. Against the $1600^2$ numerical reference, the fp64 density $L_1$ error
decreases and SSIM increases between $N=200$ and $N=400$
(Table~\ref{tab:ch5-2d-summary}); the same-resolution fp64--fp32
conservative-state $L_1$ difference at $N=400$ remains well below the
reference-scale density error.

Liska-Wendroff configuration 12 (LW12) is treated as a separate
two-dimensional Riemann problem, not as a repeat of LW3. Its initial data
contain two supersonic shock segments and two contact interfaces
\citep{liska_wendroff_2003}. Figure~\ref{fig:ch5-lw12-schlieren} shows a
different topology: a large curved shock arc reaches towards the upper-right
corner, with a second curved front and a small shear/contact roll-up near the
central interaction. Its reference comparison uses a higher-resolution $800^2$
fp64 numerical reference, not an exact solution. With that reference, the fp64 density $L_1$ error decreases and SSIM increases
between $N=200$ and $N=400$ (Table~\ref{tab:ch5-2d-summary}); the fp32--fp64
density difference at $N=400$ remains below the available reference scale,
but this ratio is not a convergence proof for the $800^2$ reference.

\section{Single- and Double-Precision Comparison}
% <<SECTION_4_BEGIN>>
Using the reference-scaled ratio defined in Equation~\ref{eq:ch4-rref},
Fig.~\ref{fig:ch5-float-double-over-reference} reports the direct IEEE
fp32--fp64 density comparison for LW3. For density, $R_\rho=4.47\times10^{-5}$ at
$N=200$ and $R_\rho=9.25\times10^{-5}$ at $N=400$. The ratio therefore
remains far below one, but it grows with resolution because the reference error
shrinks faster than the fp32--fp64 drift. The same pattern appears in the
second two-dimensional case: for LW12 against the $800^2$ fp64 reference,
$R_\rho=4.63\times10^{-5}$ at $N=200$ and
$R_\rho=1.30\times10^{-4}$ at $N=400$.
The pressure ratios in Table~\ref{tab:ch5-2d-summary} remain below the same
available fp64-reference scale for the tested grids: LW3 gives
$R_p=1.34\times10^{-5}$ and $2.59\times10^{-5}$, while LW12 gives
$R_p=3.85\times10^{-5}$ and $1.13\times10^{-4}$ from $200^2$ to $400^2$.

The one-dimensional exact-reference comparisons give a similar scale for the selected
shock-tube tests. At $N=800$, the density fp32--fp64 gap divided by the
fp64--exact error is $1.06\times10^{-4}$ for Sod,
$1.36\times10^{-5}$ for Toro3, and $3.91\times10^{-5}$ for Toro5.
The stationary-contact pressure ratio is reported as $\infty$ because the
fp64--exact denominator is zero; this case is a degenerate-reference result,
not a quantitative precision-adequacy ratio.

These measurements show that, for the tested Euler cases, the final-time direct
fp32--fp64 density and pressure differences are smaller than the available
reference or discretisation error estimates~\citep{higham_2002}. They do not
show that fp32 is generally sufficient: the ratios are case-, field-,
resolution-, and final-time metrics, and the increasing two-dimensional ratios
show that the comparison can move as the numerical reference improves. The
localised LW12 density difference
in Fig.~\ref{fig:ch5-lw12-precision-heatmap} is consistent with precision
sensitivity in the steep pressure and density gradients of the upper-right
shock-interaction region. Those gradients drive extrema in local wave-speed
estimates, which enter the HLLC star-state and flux algebra described in
Chapter~3. The figure does not, by itself, prove that a specific HLLC branch
changed; no branch-count diagnostic is used for that figure. Virtual-precision sensitivity maps
are discussed separately in Chapter~6 rather than used as direct fp32 evidence
here.

Saved-checkpoint precision drift gives the same caution in time: for fp64--fp32
HLLC outputs, final density $L_1$ drift ranges from
$8.25\times10^{-8}$ for Sod to $2.56\times10^{-6}$ for Toro5, while Toro5
pressure reaches $1.98\times10^{-4}$. This is precision-pair drift, not
exact-error measurement.

\section{Matched CPU/GPU Comparison}
% <<SECTION_5_BEGIN>>
The matched CPU/GPU comparison asks a narrower question than the fp32--fp64
study: for a fixed case, precision, solver, and binary, does changing the
runtime device alter the saved conservative state? Table~\ref{tab:ch5-cpu-gpu}
summarises the available strict-HLLC coverage without listing each zero-valued
comparison separately. All entries use
\texttt{solver=hllc} and \texttt{STRICT\_IEEE=ON}; the strict build disables
the fast-math and fused-operation shortcuts that would otherwise make a device
comparison less controlled. The final-output comparison is byte-level for the
saved \texttt{grid.bin} files, with $L_1$, $L_\infty$, and ULP metrics computed
on the conservative state.

All 14 final-output comparisons in Table~\ref{tab:ch5-cpu-gpu} are zero in
\(L_1\), \(L_\infty\), and \(\mathrm{ULP}_{\max}\), as are the 40
saved-checkpoint comparisons for Sod, LW3, and LW12. Three structural
properties of the matched-binary configuration make this absence of
saved-state divergence the expected outcome rather than a surprise:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item the comparison is restricted to saved conservative states produced
        by matched strict-IEEE builds, in which fast-math reassociation,
        FMA contraction, denormal flush-to-zero, and approximate division
        and square root are all disabled on both the CPU and the CUDA path
        (Table~\ref{tab:ch4-strict-flags});
  \item the conserved-state write-out path carries no floating-point
        summation reduction, so the saved bytes are the result of the same
        ordered local operations on either backend;
  \item the global CFL selection in
        Equation~\ref{eq:ch4-cfl-scan} is an ordered \(\max/\min\) over
        cell-local wave-speed candidates, not an unordered sum, so the
        chosen \(\Delta t\) does not depend on block, warp, or thread order.
\end{enumerate}
The GPU is nonetheless doing live arithmetic in these runs, not echoing CPU
outputs: the supplementary CUDA flag probe at the end of this section shows
that switching from strict to fast-math CUDA controls perturbs the same
saved states by up to \(\mathrm{ULP}_{\max}\approx 30.7\) on LW3 fp32, which
would be impossible if the GPU path were inert. The zero-drift result is
therefore a statement about what the matched strict build hides, not about
whether the GPU is exercised.

The zero-drift statement is bounded to matched within-case strict-HLLC
binaries and saved conservative states; it does not cover unsaved primitive
states, wave-speed estimates, sub-step values, or other devices, compilers,
and solvers. As a CPU-only sanity check, an additional WSL/GCC strict CPU
rerun of Toro3 and Toro5 retained the same qualitative fp64-vs-fp32 ranking
and matched the existing strict CPU outputs with zero saved-state drift.
Saved-checkpoint coverage is uneven by design: Sod, LW3, and LW12 were
re-run after a uniform checkpoint-emit option was enabled, while the Toro3
and Toro5 Windows-BuildTools binaries used at validation time emitted final
output only. The final-output zero-drift result for Toro3 and Toro5 is
consistent with the saved-checkpoint behaviour of the three Linux/WSL cases,
but a future re-run with checkpoints enabled is the natural way to close
this gap.

\section{Toro2 Branch Stability}
% <<SECTION_7_BEGIN>>
Under the same CPU--fp64 toolchain and default CFL, the baseline $\leq$ branch finishes Toro~123 in roughly $0.13$~s, while the strict $<$ branch does not produce a final \texttt{grid.bin} within the $600$~s wall-clock budget. The two branches differ only in a single comparison inside the HLLC dispatcher.

The mechanism is visible analytically. Toro~123 is the symmetric near-vacuum problem with $u_L = -2$, $u_R = +2$, $\rho_L = \rho_R = \rho$ and $p_L = p_R$, hence $a_L = a_R = a$. Substituting into Eq.~\ref{eq:ch3-sstar-decomp}, the pressure jump $p_R - p_L$ vanishes and the momentum-wave terms cancel pairwise, $\rho_L u_L (S_L - u_L) - \rho_R u_R (S_R - u_R) = 2\rho a - 2\rho a = 0$, so $N_\ast = 0$ exactly by symmetry. The denominator is $D_\ast = -2\rho a$, well-conditioned and bounded away from zero. Analytically $S_\ast = 0$ from Eq.~\ref{eq:ch3-hllc-sstar}, but the computed $N_\ast$ carries rounding noise, so the sign of the computed $S_\ast$ is set by that noise.

The $\leq$ branch routes the equality $S_\ast = 0$ to one fixed flux. The strict $<$ branch sees the rounded sign of $S_\ast$ flip between sub-steps and alternates between $F_{\ast L}$ and $F_{\ast R}$; repeated alternation drives flux-update inconsistency and CFL collapse. This is consistent with the observation, but the stage-by-stage trigger is not directly logged; a stage-resolved diagnostic is deferred to Report~2. The result is a single-case observation, not a systematic study.
