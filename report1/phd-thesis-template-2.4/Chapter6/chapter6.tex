\section{Precision Adequacy and Region-Aware Diagnostics}
% <<SECTION_2_BEGIN>>
The validation results give each precision and hardware difference a scale. Exact Riemann and high-resolution numerical references enter through Chapter~4 Equation~\eqref{eq:ch4-rref}, while matched CPU/GPU runs test saved conservative outputs under a fixed within-case setup. For the non-degenerate reference comparisons, direct IEEE fp32/fp64 final-state differences stay below the available reference or discretisation scale in both density and pressure. The largest reported density ratio, \(R_\rho \approx 1.30\times10^{-4}\) for Liska-Wendroff configuration 12 (LW12) at \(400^2\), remains informative, and the corresponding pressure ratio is comparable at \(R_p \approx 1.13\times10^{-4}\). The density difference occurs with a localised upper-right shock-interaction structure in Fig.~\ref{fig:ch5-lw12-precision-heatmap}. That localisation suggests sensitivity where reconstructed states feed HLLC wave-speed or flux decisions, without proving that a specific HLLC branch changed. The stationary-contact infinite ratio is a degenerate-reference case, because its denominator is zero. The following diagnostics locate precision pressure rather than introduce a separate IEEE-fp32 claim.

Parker's Monte Carlo Arithmetic perturbs floating-point operations to form an ensemble, and Denis et al.'s Verificarlo applies that idea to compiled programs \citep{parker_1997,denis_etal_2016}. In this report, the MCA ensemble gives the per-cell distribution needed to locate sensitivity rather than only estimate a single global rounding scale. Verificarlo MCA gives an ensemble spread per cell; before extending to LW3, the same idea is read off a one-dimensional Sod diagnostic. Figure~\ref{fig:ch6-sigma-fp} then uses the \(\sigma_{\mathrm{FP}}\) local roundoff scale of Chapter~4 Section~4.3: for both HLLC and Rusanov, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{FP}}\) falls as virtual mantissa precision rises. Lower or similar Rusanov values are read as the combined effect of a different arithmetic path and extra diffusion smoothing gradients, not as an accuracy ranking or as reproducibility drift. The virtual-precision labels are diagnostic mantissa settings rather than IEEE storage formats (see Section~2.4).

The loss-of-significance score LoSoS gives a complementary view of the same LW3 density samples. Figure~\ref{fig:ch6-losos-quantiles} reports quantiles rather than the worst cell: for HLLC the median LoSoS rises with virtual precision, while lower quantiles stay closer to the front-sensitive part of the field (Fig.~\ref{fig:ch6-losos-quantiles}). The raw-field quantiles use only two or three samples per virtual precision, so the figure is a spatial diagnostic, not a confidence-bounded distribution estimate.

The required-significant-digits threshold \(s_{\mathrm{req}}\) turns LoSoS into a regional margin. Figure~\ref{fig:ch6-region-losos} plots available digits at \texttt{p32} minus \(s_{\mathrm{req}}\). For HLLC at \texttt{p32}, the smooth interior keeps a positive margin while the shock-front band is negative; the transition/contact band sits in between (Fig.~\ref{fig:ch6-region-losos}). The same pattern appears in Fig.~\ref{fig:ch6-noise-error}: for HLLC, the fraction of cells with MCA noise above the reference-error scale drops monotonically with virtual precision (Fig.~\ref{fig:ch6-noise-error}). This reading is consistent with the LW12 localisation in Chapter~5, but remains bounded to the tested precisions, cases, and LW3 MCA grid.

\paragraph{SNR framing.}
The $\sigma_{\mathrm{FP}}$, LoSoS, $s_{\mathrm{req}}$, and noise-to-error
views all reduce to a local signal-to-noise comparison. Defining a per-cell
signal-to-noise ratio as the ratio of local reference error to MCA rounding
noise,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ch6-snr}
  \mathrm{SNR}(\mathbf{x})
  = \frac{|U_{\mathrm{fp64}}(\mathbf{x}) - U_{\mathrm{ref}}(\mathbf{x})|}{\sigma_{\mathrm{FP}}(\mathbf{x})},
\end{equation}
the noise-to-error heatmap of Fig.~\ref{fig:ch6-noise-error} is the indicator
$\mathrm{SNR}<1$ over the LW3 grid, while the required-digits threshold gives
$s_{\mathrm{req}} \approx \log_{10}(\mathrm{SNR}^{-1}_{\mathrm{target}})$ when
the target ratio is fixed across the field~\citep{sohier_etal_2021}.
Figure~\ref{fig:ch6-region-snr} shows the same SNR view split by smooth,
transition, and shock-front regions: precision-limited cells
($\mathrm{SNR}<1$) concentrate where the limited reconstruction feeds the
HLLC star-state algebra, and the precision-limited fraction shrinks
monotonically from \texttt{p8} to \texttt{p32}.

\section{Hardware and Implementation Sensitivity}
% <<SECTION_3_BEGIN>>
The matched strict-HLLC CPU/GPU evidence is a saved-output statement. At final time, Sod, Toro3, Toro5, Liska-Wendroff configuration 3 (LW3), and Liska-Wendroff configuration 12 (LW12) show no saved-state divergence between CPU and GPU in both fp32 and fp64, with \(L_1=0\), \(L_\infty=0\), and \(\mathrm{ULP}_{\max}=0\). Saved-checkpoint comparisons on Sod, LW3, and LW12 add the same zero-drift result for checkpoint files; Toro3 and Toro5 have final-output evidence only. These results do not establish equality of all intermediate stage values inside a time step, or equivalence across arbitrary compilers, devices, and build controls. The toolchain split gives bounded comparability rather than a within-case weakness: Toro3 and Toro5 use Windows BuildTools, while Sod, LW3, and LW12 use Linux/WSL; each within-case CPU/GPU comparison uses one matched binary/configuration.

The other axes show implementation or method sensitivity rather than a general hardware effect. In CPU double, the largest non-stationary compiler variation is O2 versus Ofast-fastmath for Toro3 and Toro5, while the branch-rule entries test an implementation choice whose effect is smaller or absent in completing comparisons. The fp32 compiler matrix is precision sensitivity under compiler flags, not hardware evidence. HLLC versus Rusanov, limiter variation, and the shock-bubble support packet are method-sensitivity checks; they support interpretation but are not part of the five-case validation claim. The shock-bubble packet adds an \(800\times200\) fp64 CPU HLLC morphology cross-check beyond the \(400\times100\) run; the \(1600\times400\) self-convergence row did not complete and no strict CUDA build exists, so no CPU/GPU comparison is made. The supplementary GPU flag probe gives the build-control boundary on the zero-drift CPU/GPU statement: changing strict versus fast CUDA controls can change saved GPU states. Checkpoint histories show finite-time, case-dependent drift growth, so they support a bounded time-window reading rather than an extrapolated rate.

\section{Limitations and Report 2 Direction}
\label{sec:ch6-limits}
% <<SECTION_4_BEGIN>>
The limitations are evidence-specific:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item LW12 uses an \(800^2\) fp64 numerical reference, not an exact
        solution, and LW3 relies on high-resolution self-convergence
        between \(400^2\), \(800^2\), and \(1600^2\) fp64 grids.
  \item Compiler evidence uses one compiler family inside each case (GCC~13
        for the Linux/WSL cases, MSVC Build Tools for Toro3 and Toro5),
        with no cross-compiler matrix.
  \item Hardware evidence uses one NVIDIA RTX 4060 Laptop GPU and one
        x86\_64 host; cross-device portability is not claimed.
  \item Verificarlo \texttt{p32} is a virtual mantissa precision, distinct
        from IEEE binary32, and the region-margin diagnostics use small
        MCA sample counts.
  \item Toro2 with the strict \(<\) HLLC branch did not complete inside
        600\,s while the \(\leq\) branch did, so it is kept as an
        observed branch-stability result rather than a proved mechanism.
  \item Limiter and shock-bubble results support method-sensitivity
        interpretation but do not replace the validation suite.
\end{enumerate}
The validated scope is ideal-gas Euler only: matched CPU/GPU evidence covers
five selected cases, and no MHD validation is claimed.

Report~2 should extend the framework to ideal-MHD tests with divergence
control, treating Dedner-type cleaning \citep{dedner_2002} or constrained
transport \citep{evans_hawley_1988} as explicit numerical choices and HLLD
\citep{miyoshi_kusano_2005} as a candidate MHD Riemann solver. The brief
asks whether discrepancies between implementations grow over time, with
chaotic MHD problems flagged as the limiting case. Quantifying that growth
connects to the dynamical-systems framework for sensitive dependence on
initial conditions: the Lyapunov-exponent estimate from a time series
\citep{wolf_etal_1985} measures the average exponential separation rate of
nearby trajectories, and the ergodic theory of chaotic attractors
\citep{eckmann_ruelle_1985} explains why the long-time observable is that
rate rather than any single finite-time drift. The Chapter~5
drift-time-series figure is a finite-time precursor of that analysis;
Report~2 will pick it up on the MHD test set.
% <<SECTION_4_END>>
