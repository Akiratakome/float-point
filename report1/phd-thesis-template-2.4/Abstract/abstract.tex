\begin{abstract}
This report measures how precision and hardware/backend choices affect a
stand-alone CPU/CUDA Euler high-resolution shock-capturing code. The
solver uses a nominally second-order MUSCL--Hancock finite-volume scheme
with the HLLC Riemann solver. Five test cases span one and two dimensions. Sod, Toro3, and Toro5 are
checked against exact Riemann references; Liska--Wendroff configurations~3
and 12 sit against \(1600^2\) and \(800^2\) fp64 numerical references. The experimental matrix compares real
fp32/fp64 builds, matched strict-IEEE HLLC CPU/GPU saved outputs,
selected compiler, branch-rule, and solver variations, and Verificarlo
Monte~Carlo arithmetic diagnostics.

Three findings stand out. First, the matched within-binary strict-IEEE HLLC
CPU/GPU saved-output comparisons hit \(L_1=0\), \(L_\infty=0\), and
\(\mathrm{ULP}_{\max}=0\) on saved conservative states, covering final
outputs and checkpoints across all five cases. Zero drift is claimed only for
saved states, not for unsaved intermediate values. Second, direct
fp32/fp64 differences remain below the available reference or
discretisation scale in every tested case, with the largest density
reference-scaled ratio reaching \(1.30\times10^{-4}\) for LW12 at
\(400^2\). Third, implementation sensitivity is axis-dependent: \texttt{-O2}
and \texttt{-O3} are bit-identical where tested, while fast-math perturbs
non-stationary saved states. Together these results form an auditable Euler
baseline for the later MHD extension and identify reduced-precision,
implementation, and matched-binary caveats.
\end{abstract}
