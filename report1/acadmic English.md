Introduction
The aims of this Learning Object
Academic English is a vast subject and there are many different ideas about what exactly good academic English is. In this Learning Object we focus mainly on written academic English from the point of view of strategies, structure and style.

The main aim is to invite you to think. This Learning Object is designed to help you explore questions about what the essential characteristics of good academic English are. We do not attempt to cover every last detail. Rather we focus on those areas which, from long experience, we have found to be the most productive and thought-provoking for international postgraduate students coming to start work in Cambridge.

In this Learning Object you will analyse and explore:
a variety of examples of good, and sometimes not so good, practice in order to identify key principles underlying good academic writing
the relationship between the demands posed by academic expectations and conventions and the kind of language used to fulfil them
practical pathways and techniques to expand the range and appropriacy of the language you use in academic writing whatever your discipline

 The structure of this Learning Object
There are three parts to this Learning Object: Strategies, Structure and Style.

Strategies
In Strategies we identify how some of the key principles in academic writing are realised. The four principles focused on are Clarity, Objectivity, Precision and Honesty.

In the first section we look at Audience Expectations and Conventions: Clarity and examine the differences between strategies characteristic of academic style and journalistic style. We look at organisation and how this reflects the need for clarity.

In the second section on Objectivity we look at the question of impersonality and whether we should use ‘I’ or ‘we’ or the passive voice.

In the third section we see how the principle of Precision can be realised through the use of a cautious approach and by using tentative language.

In the last section we look at strategies used to ensure Honesty in relation to the use of other people’s ideas and work. Here we see how this is realised in terms of approach and use of language.

Structure
Structure is divided into two sections. In the first section, The Relationship between Structure and Language, we take a genre approach and look at two extracts from academic papers: the end of an introduction and the results section of a scientific paper. We find that predictability in structure is matched by predictable patterns in language use.

In the second section, Aspects of Argument, we look at the structure of argument and in particular at the organisation of paragraphs. We focus first on the structure of paragraphs and then examine the relationship between coherence and cohesion. A technique on how to learn linking language is then given followed by an approach to identifying and learning useful language in academic texts.

Style
In Style there is a particular focus on practical strategies for expanding and refining aspects of academic language. We divide this into four sections.

In the first section we look at Features of Academic Style and focus on some of the stylistic advantages of using the passive voice. We also examine how learning sentence structures using the Phrasebank website can be enhanced by mining journal papers for relevant examples to strengthen usage.

In the second section we look at how to learn Academic Collocations in a systematic way by using journal papers and Google Scholar.

In the third section the subject of Academic Bad Language is raised and we explore two very different types of inappropriate language in academic writing.

Finally, in order to practise some aspects of appropriacy of style in Stylistic Translations we provide exercises for students of the Arts and Humanities, Social sciences and the sciences.

Text 1: Academic Introduction
Rising atmospheric CO2 from the burning of fossil fuels and deforestation affects marine systems in several ways. Through its effect on the global climate, it increases sea surface temperatures (now 0.7 °C higher than in pre-industrial times) and intensifies storm and rainfall variability, altering salinity and the terrestrial runoff of nutrients and sediments8. It also causes profound changes in sea water chemistry. Atmospheric CO2 concentrations of ~390 ppm already exceed by 50–100% the historic envelope of 200–300 ppm in the past >2 million years9. The resulting increased partial pressure of carbon dioxide (pCO2) in sea water has already reduced mean surface seawater pH by 0.1, thus lowering carbonate ion concentrations by 30 μmol kg−1, and the saturation state of sea water for calcium carbonate minerals (Ω) by ~15%, although the magnitude of these effects varies regionally and with latitude1,5,10,11.


The declining pH, termed ‘ocean acidification’, is predicted to have profound implications for marine ecosystems because carbonate ions are an essential substrate for biotic calcification. Coral reefs are of particular concern because their many tens of thousands of species ultimately depend on the structural complexity derived from the corals’ carbonate skeletons5,6. However, specific knowledge about the capacity of reef ecosystems to acclimatize and/or adapt to long-term exposure to lowered pH (increased pCO2 and reduced Ω) remains inadequate. Much of our understanding stems from short-term laboratory perturbation experiments of individual organisms or from deterministic models. Perturbation experiments report variable and sometimes severe responses in many marine plants, invertebrates and vertebrates at lowered pH, such as declining calcification, altered physiologies and some effects on survival2,3,4. Although laboratory experiments are indispensable, most are too brief for full organism acclimatization to occur, and co-limiting factors (for example nutrients, currents and irradiance) are difficult to simulate ex situ12,13. Experiments also provide little information about processes leading to ecosystem adaptation, such as altered reproduction, competition, food webs and disease susceptibility, or genetic adaptation. There is therefore a great need for empirical data documenting the long-term effects of ocean acidification on marine ecosystems acclimatized to high pCO2, as found around submarine CO2 vents. Recently, changes in shallow-water marine rocky shore ecosystems have been investigated at volcanic CO2 vents in the Mediterranean, documenting major declines in many calcifying and non-calcifying organisms and increases in macroalgae and seagrasses at reduced seawater pH (refs 7, 14).


Here we report the effects of natural in situ exposure to elevated seawater pCO2 on tropical coral reef communities, coral growth, recruitment, seagrasses and sedimentary properties. The study is based on field investigations of clear-water coral reefs and seagrass communities around three cool volcanic seeps of ~99%  CO2 gas, and at three adjacent control sites with similar geomorphology, seawater temperature and salinity, that fringe the D’Entrecastraux Islands, Milne Bay Province, Papua New Guinea (Supplementary Figs S1, S2, Table S1).

 Objectivity
We have been introduced to the idea that academic writing should be objective. It is the evidence or the argument that is judged and not the individual. One way to achieve a sense of objectivity is through impersonality. “ ‘I’ and ‘we’ are subjective and personal so keep them out of academic writing”. This is an idea suggested to many young students. This section examines this idea and suggests that while the use of the passive voice often helps the reader focus on the material there are occasions when using ‘I’ and ‘we’ serve the aims of objectivity very well and can also make academic writing more readable.

 Precision
The principle focused on in this section is precision and how this can be achieved through caution. Why do academics choose to be cautious in their approach and language? One reason is that they wish to be as precise as possible about how certain they are. Another reason is they wish to be cautious or tentative about the value or significance of results or conclusions. This has interesting consequences for choices made concerning sentence structure and vocabulary in academic texts.

 Honesty
This last section in Strategies is concerned with the principle of honesty. Academic disciplines have developed complex systems of referencing to ensure that an original author’s work is protected. Each time the words or ideas from an author’s work are used by another writer that writer must show the nature and extent of that usage. To fail to do so can lead to a charge of plagiarism, effectively a form of intellectual theft. This section does not attempt to deal with referencing systems but rather looks at the consequences of using the work of others in terms of strategies and language use.

Structure and Language
Research students have to do a great deal of reading. While the content of what they read may be varied and difficult one thing that provides enormous help is the predictability of academic writing. In any given field there are very strong family likenesses in the structure of journal papers in particular. We find the same things going on in the same places. The writers are constrained by the conventions of academic writing to focus, for instance, on their specific aims at the end of their introductions. Sections of academic papers, particularly in the sciences, are very similar, not in the information they provide, but in the order and manner in which that information is given. This approach to looking at the predictable relationship between structure and language is very useful for us.

This section is divided into two parts. In the first part, The Relationship between Structure and Language, we take a genre approach and look at two sections of academic papers: the end of an introduction and the results section of a scientific paper. We find that predictability in structure is matched by predictable patterns in language use.

In the second part, Aspects of Argument, we look at the structure of argument and in particular at the organisation of paragraphs. We focus first on the structure of paragraphs and then examine the relationship between coherence and cohesion. A technique on how to learn linking language is then given followed by an approach to identifying and learning useful language in academic texts.

Aspects of Argument
In this section we look at:

 What is a paragraph?
Many things go on in paragraphs and if we read different writers even within the same discipline we find enormous variation in the way paragraphs are used. It is therefore dangerous to be prescriptive about what the structure of a paragraph should be. There are also significant differences between what goes into paragraphs in the humanities and the sciences.

The paragraph is an essential unit of organisation of meaning or information so it is worth looking at the structure of paragraphs. As you read through the example paragraph ask yourself the following questions:

What should happen at the beginning of a paragraph?
Is all the information or argument consistently relevant?
Is there a logical order to the information or argument?
What should happen at the end of a paragraph?
Example paragraph
The analysis of oral tradition offers some hope in reconstructing earlier languages. In several parts of the world stories, sometimes hundreds of years old, are handed down in highly schematic forms and when these are finally written down elements of earlier languages are preserved. This is true of, for example, Homer’s epic poems and indeed of some Eastern European bardic works. This is not to say that such oral traditions preserve earlier languages in aspic, although in the Vedic hymns from India this is to some extent the case, it is rather to say that elements of lexis and syntax remain over long periods of time and, when identified, it is these that can aid linguists in piecing together a ‘lost’ language. An example of what can be achieved can be seen in analysis of residual lexical items. By removing modern vocabulary and by focusing on what is known as ‘basic vocabulary’, ie vocabulary least subject to change over time such as parts of the body and natural phenomena, it is possible not only to reconstruct an earlier vocabulary but to gain insights into the forms and sounds of those words. Hypotheses can then be built and theories developed. The problem, however, and this is why we can say the analysis of oral tradition only offers some hope, is that there is such a lack of this kind of evidence overall, and what we have is so patchy and fragmented, that the building of such hypotheses and theories is fraught with difficulty.

The opening sentence introduces the theme to be developed. Note that in this case the use of ‘some hope’ suggests the paragraph may develop not only ways in which oral tradition may help reconstruct earlier languages but also may focus on the limitations of using this kind of analysis.
In several parts of the world stories, sometimes hundreds of years old, are handed down in highly schematic forms and when these are finally written down elements of earlier languages are preserved.

The second sentence expands on what is meant by oral tradition and connects this to preservation of ‘elements’ of earlier languages.
This is true of, for example, Homer’s epic poems and indeed of some Eastern European bardic works.

A brief example is provided.
This is not to say that such oral traditions preserve earlier languages in aspic, although in the Vedic hymns from India this is to some extent the case, it is rather to say that elements of lexis and syntax remain over long periods of time and, when identified, it is these that can aid linguists in piecing together a ‘lost’ language.

A further clarification and a qualification are added. The writer is narrowing down to what in particular oral tradition can offer a linguist. This sets up further development of the paragraph.
An example of what can be achieved can be seen in analysis of residual lexical items.

This development comes via an example which is introduced here.
By removing modern vocabulary and by focusing on what is known as ‘basic vocabulary’, ie vocabulary least subject to change over time such as parts of the body and natural phenomena, it is possible not only to reconstruct an earlier vocabulary but to gain insights into the forms and sounds of those words.

The example provides explanation of the way a particular approach enables a linguist to begin to identify elements of the earlier language.
Hypotheses can then be built and theories developed.

The consequences of this identification are briefly mentioned.
The problem, however, and this is why we can say the analysis of oral tradition only offers some hope, is that there is such a lack of this kind of evidence overall, and what we have is so patchy and fragmented, that the building of such hypotheses and theories is fraught with difficulty.

The final sentence provides us with perspective and links to the opening of the paragraph by focusing on the limitations of the approach given. It also points forward to a probable development of this in the next paragraph.
Source - Student essay.

Final comments

Not all paragraphs you read will conform to the structure you have seen here. When paragraphs come in groups a final perspective may not be given until the end and the paragraphs may be seen more as building blocks in a larger structure. Ultimately only broad guidelines can be given about the structure of paragraphs and it may be useful to ask yourself the following questions when you construct your own:

Do I make clear what the focus of the paragraph is?
What exactly is established in the paragraph?
Is there a clear, logical development of sentences?
Is everything in the paragraph directly relevant to the main focus?
Would the reader benefit if long paragraphs were broken into shorter ones?
