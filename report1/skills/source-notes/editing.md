Approaches to Editing
Academic Development and Training for International Students: Editing
General Introduction
We spend a lot of our time writing. When we think we have finished a first draft another process begins. This is the editing process. It begins with revising and reviewing the content, organisation and focus and once this is done we move on to editing and polishing, to improve the quality of communication and style. Finally, the laborious task of proof-reading begins and it may take several attempts before we get it right. Overall, we move from the big picture to the tiny details, from the macro to the micro. In reality these three steps may not occur in a strict sequence but may overlap each other. What we can say for certain is that the final product may look very different from the first draft.

For those of us who plan very well the editing process is correspondingly easier. The kind of questions and principles we apply to the formation of a piece of work, after all, are very similar to those we apply in the revising and reviewing process. Understanding, for example, the audience’s expectations, the conventions and criteria we are working with and having good models to learn from all help us in planning and editing our work. An appreciation of cultural differences in academic writing can be important and can affect the relationship we build with a reader.

The aim of this Learning Object is to focus, in a very practical way, on the middle phase of the editing process, that of improving the quality of communication and style.

An introduction to Editing
It is always easier to edit someone else’s work than our own. Overfamiliarity with our own work can lead sometimes to a kind of blindness concerning its faults. This is compounded by the difficulties in editing on screen. A mass of words, not being able to see what came before or what comes after and the fact that on our computer screen it just looks so good all contribute to making it more difficult to spot the problems that a good editor would see. We need distance. This can be provided in part by leaving a gap between writing and editing: we can come at it ‘cold’ and be more objective. In an ideal world we would be well-organised enough to manage this. For most of us this just doesn’t happen. Therefore we need techniques to help us edit better, edit ‘smart’.

One simple technique is to make the text look different so that it no longer looks ‘ours’. This can be done by changing the font, the colour of the text and the formatting. This helps create an illusion of distance and can be very helpful if it is necessary to edit on screen. Ultimately though, we need more thorough and searching methods of assessing the quality of our writing and of spotting the weaknesses hidden in our paragraphs and sentences.

This learning object examines the advantages of two editing techniques and rather than simply describing them we actively take you through the processes involved. We hope that by doing this you will appreciate at a deeper level not only how they work but how they can work for you. In the final section we look at two key issues in the editing process which have a significant effect on the quality of our communication: hedging and nominalisation.

Editing Technique 1
Paragraph Splitting
Introduction
The principle behind paragraph splitting is simple. Take a paragraph, copy and paste it onto another document, and separate all the sentences from each other by hitting the return key twice whenever you see a full stop. You will see each sentence of the paragraph in isolation.

Here we attempt to demonstrate how useful paragraph splitting is by taking a paragraph and using the technique to improve it in a number of ways. There are 3 stages corresponding to 3 versions of the paragraph as it goes through the editing process. In Version 1 we see the original paragraph and use the technique to identify the problems which need to be solved. You can compare your thoughts with those of a supervisor. In Version 2 we make improvements and focus on those editing questions which will help us take the editing process further. In Version 3 we see a much improved version, think about why it is better and ask a few more editing questions. At the end you can listen to the supervisor commenting on what has been achieved and reflect on what kind of questions facilitate good editing.

Editing Technique 2
Screen reading v Reading aloud
Introduction
The second editing technique we will look at is reading aloud. There are good reasons to do this. Firstly, reading on screen involves only a form of visual checking and our eyes are apt to move around the page leading to the tendency to ‘miss’ things which need attention. Secondly, and very importantly, reading aloud involves a deeper and more thorough kind of processing. It forces us to notice things which we do not always notice when reading silently. It encourages us to be aware of faults in coherence as well as style or accuracy. Here we attempt to demonstrate how useful reading aloud is by taking a paragraph and using the technique to improve it in a number of ways. As with paragraph splitting there are 3 stages corresponding to 3 versions of the paragraph as it goes through the editing process. In Stage 1 we see the original paragraph, written by a non-native English speaker, and use the technique to identify the problems which need to be solved. You can compare your thoughts with those of a supervisor. In Stage 2 we make improvements and focus on those editing questions which will help us take the editing process further. In Stage 3 we see a much improved version, think about why it is better and ask a few more editing questions. At the end you can listen to the supervisor commenting on what has been achieved and reflect on what kind of questions facilitate good editing.

Were Neandertals capable of ritual behaviour?
Neandertals probably played a small part, from a genetic point of view, in the ancestry of modern Europeans. They were a different species and died out around 30,000 years ago. Beyond a range of physical differences, the degree of similarity in other senses between Neandertals and modern humans has been a matter of debate for decades. They made sophisticated stone tools and some experts have suggested they had culture, language and that most human of attributes, consciousness. How does one obtain proof of consciousness? Evidence of Neandertal art is virtually non-existent and what does exist is controversial. If evidence of ritual behaviour could be established this would go far to confirming a mind capable of reflection, deep emotion and an ability to translate this into symbolic thought and actions. Ritual behaviour, in other words, would be an index of consciousness and would have considerable ramifications for the way we view the ‘humanity’ of Neandertals. The best evidence of ritual behaviour so far found is in a Neandertal ‘grave’ in the Shanidar cave in Iraq containing a skeleton and various types of flower and herb pollen. Is this evidence of Neandertals placing flowers and plants of symbolic importance on the body of a loved one: a very ‘human’ burial ceremony?

Hedging
 What is Hedging? An Example
When reviewing and editing it is necessary to make decisions about your stance on a particular subject, or the strength of the claims that you are making. This is where hedging, or tentative language, is very useful.

In order to appreciate the importance of hedging, let’s look at two examples of the same sentence.

Our findings suggest that to some extent global warming can be attributed to the effects of human activity.

Our findings demonstrate that global warming is caused by human activity.

Hedging protects academic writers from making statements beyond their level of certainty.  This is one reason we hedge in academic writing. In fact we hedge for a wide variety of reasons and not all are good. There are good questions to ask on hedging in the editing process:

Nominalisation
 What are Nominalisations?
Nominalisations are noun phrases derived from nouns, adjectives but, most often, verbs. What usually happens is a verb is turned into a noun and this changes an action into a concept or thing and, for better or worse, they are very popular in academic writing. For some the use of nominalisations is a characteristic of academic style. As you work through this section try to decide whether they are helpful to a reader and how far and when you want to use them.

 How to Recognise Nominalisation
Look at the following pairs of sentences. The sentences in blue contain highlighted nominalisations.

The intention of the committee is to audit the records.

The committee intends to audit the records.

Comment: The nominalisation is the subject of an empty verb such as be, seems, has etc.

The agency conducted an investigation into the matter.

The agency investigated the matter.

Comment: The nominalisation follows an empty verb.

Our loss in sales was a result of their expansion of outlets.

We lost sales because they expanded their outlets.

Comment: One nominalisation is the subject of an empty verb and a second nominalisation follows it.

There is no need for our further study of this problem.

We need not study this problem further.

Comment: A nominalisation follows there is or there are.

We did a review of the evolution of the brain.

We reviewed how the brain evolved.

Comment: Two or three nominalisations in a row are joined by prepositions.

One observation that can be made is that the sentences in red contain more active verbs and these move the meaning forward. Sentences with nominalisations, frequently found with ‘empty’ verbs, are more static. This is not to say we should avoid using nominalisations as they can have a valid purpose. Below is a summary of some of the key effects of using sentences with nominalisations:

Nominalisations…

Convey an objective or impersonal tone
Enable a writer to avoid repeating verbs in a text and create a more complex sentence structure
(Can) make a text more concise – it can concentrate information into fewer words
Make a text more abstract, and more formal
(Can) make your writing sound more ‘academic’
 Nominalisation in Longer Sentences
Compare these two sentences. Which do you prefer and why?

The Federalists’ argument in regard to the destabilization of government by popular democracy was based on their belief in the tendency of factions to further their self-interest at the expense of the common good.

The Federalists argued that popular democracy destabilized government because they believed that factions tended to further their self-interest at the expense of the common good.


Supervisor’s Comments
The Federalists’ argument in regard to the destabilization of government by popular democracy was based on their belief in the tendency of factions to further their self-interest at the expense of the common good.

The sentence’s characters are not subjects. Its simple subject is argument, but the characters (italicised) are Federalists, popular democracy, government and factions.

The Federalists’ argument in regard to the destabilization of government by popular democracy WAS BASED on their belief in the tendency of factions to further their self-interest at the expense of the common good.

Most of the actions (bold) are not verbs but abstract nouns. Notice how long the subject is and how little meaning is expressed in its main verb.

The Federalists ARGUED that popular democracy DESTABILIZSED government because they BELIEVED that factions TENDED TO FURTHER their self-interest at the expense of the common good.

Readers think this one is clearer for two reasons:

The characters (italicised) are subjects, and
The actions (bold) are verbs (capitalised).
 Verbs and Actions
A sentence seems clear when its important actions are in the verbs. Compare the effect of using nouns and verbs in the following two versions of the same sentence:

Our lack of data prevented evaluation of UN actions in targeting funds to areas most in need of assistance.

Because we lacked data, we could not evaluate whether the UN had targeted funds to areas that most needed assistance.

Readers will think your writing is dense if you use lots of abstract nouns, especially those derived from verbs and adjectives (nouns ending in –tion, -ment, -ence etc.,) especially when you make those abstract nouns the subjects of verbs.

 The Dangers of Nominalisation
No element of style more characterizes turgid writing, writing that feels abstract, indirect, and difficult, than lots of nominalisations, especially as the subjects of verbs.

The outsourcing of high-tech work to Asia by corporations means the loss of jobs for many American workers.

An abstract noun is the subject and there are 10 words before we reach the verb

Many middle-class American workers are losing their jobs because corporations are outsourcing their high-tech work to Asia.

Why is this sentence more immediate, even more dramatic, than the version in blue?


What happens when you rely on verbs?
Your sentences are more concrete

There was an affirmative decision for expansion.

The Director decided to expand the programme.

Your sentences are more concise

A revision of the programme will result in increases in the efficiency of servicing of clients.

If we revise the programme, we can serve clients more efficiently.

The logic of your sentences is clearer

Our more effective presentation of our study resulted in our success, despite an earlier start by others.

Although others started earlier, we succeeded because we presented our study more effectively.

The question is whether you want your writing to be concrete, concise and clear, and, if not, why not.

 Understanding the Importance of Characters
Look at the following two sentences:

The CIA feared the president would recommend to Congress that it reduce its budget.

The CIA had fears that the president would send a recommendation to Congress that it make a reduction in its budget

Most readers think the first is clearer than the second, but not by much.

Compare the second to this one though:

The fear of the CIA was that a recommendation from the president to Congress would be for a reduction in its budget.

Most readers think that this one is much less clear than either of the other two. Why?


Supervisor’s Comments
The CIA feared the president would recommend to Congress that it reduce its budget.

The CIA had fears that the president would send a recommendation to Congress that it make a reduction in its budget.

In both of these the important characters are short, specific subjects of verbs.

The fear of the CIA was that a recommendation from the president to Congress would be for a reduction in its budget.

But in the third sentence the two subjects are not concrete characters but abstractions.

REMEMBER:

Readers want actions in verbs, but they want characters as subjects even more.

We create a problem for readers when, for no good reason, we do not name characters in subjects or, worse, delete them entirely.

It is important to express actions in verbs, the first principle of a clear style is:

Make the subjects of most of your verbs the main characters in your story.
