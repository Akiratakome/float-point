How to write introductions
Overview
This learning object focuses on how to write introductions to academic papers and books. It offers some advice on the key elements normally found in an introduction and on structuring the introduction appropriately.

Following on from aspects of structure and strategy the object looks at some key language features normally found in introductions and provides some opportunity for practice. The section on Literature Review looks at the role of literative reviews and focuses on language used to report and evaluate the views of others.

Finally, a comparison with a text written by the same author for the general public allows for comparison in style and language between introductions intended for a specialist academic readership and introductions aimed at the general public.

The introduction to Milford Wolpoff’s The Place of the Neandertals in Human Evolution is provided as an example of a substantial introduction to an academic text. Annotations highlight the key features.

playmute
 There are many kinds of academic paper. While there will, of course, be differences between essays of varying lengths and journal articles and dissertations etc most introductions in most disciplines culminate in a clear statement of the purpose of that piece of writing. Typically, the introduction will begin by placing the subject of the paper in its context and will, then, try to demonstrate both the need for and the significance of the study. This need might be based on a gap in knowledge, an extension or refinement of current knowledge or even a disagreement with current knowledge or correction to it. Key areas for discussion will be foreshadowed and there may well be a review of the literature in a sense to locate and justify the study. The main point is that by the time we get to the end of the introduction we have a clear view of exactly WHY the paper has been written and what it sets out to achieve. In fact only by having this can the reader actually judge the value and significance of the paper itself.

 It narrows the focus from the subject as a whole to the specific question the paper is addressing

playmute
 The narrowing principle is common to all introductions. The reader needs to know how and where this particular study is going to fit into what is already known. This is the context. As the precise nature of the study is revealed in relation to the wider context the contribution and value to that context will become clearer and clearer. There is some variation in the order writers do things. Some, for example, state the needs for the study early on while others place it after the review of the literature. In any case, by the time the reader gets to the end of the introduction it should be seen that each paragraph in some way contributes to this process of creating a sharper and clearer understanding of the subject to be studied, its purpose and its scope.

playmute
 I should say first that a literature review is only necessary is some types of academic paper. In most cases it is included in longer research based papers but rarely in essays. Dissertations and PhD reports and theses usually have them. Essentially, they have a double purpose. First, to provide academic context so the academic reader can place or locate this work within the body of previous work. Second, it becomes part of the process of identifying the need for further study and in so doing justifies the study itself. For those first having to write a literature review the main fault at student level is that they are often too inclusive and not sufficiently related to the study itself – ie they are not used to pinpoint areas requiring study clearly enough. A really good literature review is selective and critical in focus and therefore always highly relevant to the study being written up.

playmute
 I think by the end of the introduction the reader should have a very clear idea of what the focus and aims of the paper are and also why it is worthy of being read. It is also an academic courtesy because without all this the reader will not be able to judge properly the merits of the paper, the merits of its ideas, of the conclusions. Does the writer really achieve their aims? Does the writer really fulfill what they set out to achieve? How, when the reader reads the concluding section will they be able to judge the results or quality of argument if there are no clear criteria or aims? Having said this, you will find that different types of academic paper do things somewhat differently. Specialist scientific studies, those particularly narrow in focus, tend not to set out a ‘thesis’ but rather relate the work to an ongoing context of research. The placement of the study is sufficient. Some writers and journals set out the structure of their study at the end of the introduction, others do not. It has to be remembered that in dealing with academic writing we are dealing with a very broad subject. Different disciplines have different ways of going about their work and newcomers to these disciplines need to familiarise themselves with this.

In this paper I address the place of the Neandertals in human evolution by examining the current status of ideas regarding the relations of the Neandertals to the post-Neandertal populations of Europe. I propose to do this by first discussing the total replacement hypothesis in terms of the genetic and morphological bases for invasion leading to replacement without significant hybridization. I will then examine the evidence relating to the less extreme competing hypotheses of demic diffusion, invasion, and/or gene flow with significant admixture, as the source of the post-Neandertal Europeans. The intent is to provide a reasonable and defensible interpretation of the roles of gene flow, selection, and drift in the appearance of the post-Neandertal Europeans. Ultimately, if the hypothesis of complete replacement (i.e. without significant admixture between aboriginal and invading populations) can be rejected, the relation of the Neandertals and the post-Neandertal Europeans must be described in terms of a transition, and this opens the door for an examination of the adaptive, demographic and reproductive changes that accompanied it and oriented its rate and direction.

How to write introductions
Reporting and evaluating in literature review
Reporting and evaluating the views of others is an important aspect of academic writing. Many introductions contain a literature review giving a critical overview of the current state of debate and the field under discussion. The extract below provides a typical example of part of a literature review. First of all read through the extract and note down the verbs, phrases and sentence structures used in reporting and evaluating.
The bases for the phylogenetic disagreements of today span only the last several decades. Theoretical perspectives have brought into focus the roles of population invasion, gene flow, and selection changes in producing the morphology of the post-Neandertal Europeans. These theoretical perspectives can be traced to the first influences of the synthetic theory of evolution on paleoanthroplogy, as specifically applied to the Neandertal problem. This influence was clearly evident in a seminal paper on the evolution of Neandertals in Europe and western Asia, published in 1951 by F.C. Howell, examining evolutionary trends within the Neandertals. Because the direction of their evolution seemed to be away from the modern European condition, especially with the (then thought to be earlier) Skhul remains at their base, Howell concluded that the classic Neandertals of western Europe were peripheral to the mainstream of human evolution. Reasoning that the presence of numerous autapomorphic characters precluded a classic Neandertal ancestry for later European populations, he argued that while the classic Neandertals evolved in western Europe, "at the same time modern man was developing further to the east" (1951:412). It was Howell’s position that the evolutionary direction leading to so unique a set of features could only develop with genetic isolation, and he sought to show (1952) that the Neandertal populations of western Europe were indeed isolated by the pattern of glaciation during Wurm II.

In a more general restatement of these arguments, Howell (1957) used a larger Neandertal sample to buttress his conclusion that modern populations evolved only from the earlier Neandertals. His discussion of the more fragmentary eastern and central European remains concluded that

East-central and eastern Europe were not particularly isolated during the Early Last Glacial….there was broad racial continuity at least as far east as the Crimea and southward into the Levant. (1957:337)

The Levant specimen reflecting this continuity was the female from Tabun. It was this group that, in his view, provided the ancestry for the later Europeans of the Upper Paleolithic.

…important structural changes were taking place in the human populations of the Levant during the Early Last Pluvial…these were of primary significance for the evolutionary origin of subsequent anatomically modern peoples of the European Upper Paleolithic. (1957:339)

Howell advocated a generalized Neandertal ancestry for the modern Europeans in these publications, for instance in his phyletic summary diagram (1951: Figure 4) showing specimens such as Ehringsdorf and Saccopastore as immediately ancestral to (what he called) the Mount Carmel population, which in turn he regarded as ancestral to modern Europeans. However, he was opposed to the interpretation of a "Neandertal phase" in the evolution of all modern populations, because of "the marked variability from one {archaic} group to another" (1957:343). In fact, he stated (1957:331), "This point of view has yet to be meaningfully expressed in terms of modern evolutionary theory."

In papers following, both C.S.Coon (1962) and C.L.Brace (1962a, 1964) attempted exactly such an expression. Just as Howell did in his emphasis on regional variation, Coon’s thinking reflected the later interpretations of F.Weidenreich (1947a). Weidenreich (1943b) had first regarded the western European classic Neandertals as ancestors of modern Europeans. But by four years later (1947a) he had dropped all mention of the western sample from his discussion of the problem and derived modern Europeans from the classic Neandertals of the Levant (meaning Tabun) via the Skhul group. While Coon followed Weidenriech in emphasizing the distinctly geographic, or regional, pattern of human evolution (see Wolpoff et al. 1984), his explanation for temporal patterning differed from Weidenreich’s orthogenic one (Weidenreich 1947b; Wolpoff 1985). Coon applied the synthetic theory of evolution, as he understood it, to this problem and approached the explanation of common phases in human evolution by regarding each of the phases as temporal species, separated by distinct boundaries ("thresholds of speciation"). In this context, Coon (1962, 1982) regarded the Neandertals as an evolutionary static, cold-adapted branch of the Caucasoids, absorbed and extinguished by the invasion of more modern Caucasoid populations from the Levant.

Brace (1962b, 1964, 1966,1979) also reflected Weidenreich’s earlier (1943b) views, and brought into a more modern context an explanatory interpretation of the Late Pleistocene fossil record first expressed in detail in publications from central Europe by Schliz (1909), Gorjanovic-kramberger (1918, and see Radovcic 1985), and Hrdlicka (1929, 1930). Specifically following Hrdlicka (1930), he proposed a worldwide Neandertal stage to human evolution (Brace 1967). Brace was not alone in this; many authors of the time regarded archaic hominids outside of Europe as Neandertal variants; for instance, von Koenigswald (1958) considered the Solo hominids as "tropical Neandertals." In Brace’s view the western European Neandertals differed from the more modern Europeans that followed them in time mainly because of their greater skeletal robustness and large anterior tooth size with supporting facial architecture. This set the stage for his hypothesis of how the Neandertals evolved into these succeeding populations. He regarded the reductions in masticatory robustness and in the size of the anterior teeth as the result of replacing tooth use with a more efficient technology (an idea proposed earlier by both Gorjanovic-Kramberger {1904} and Hrdlicka {1911, 1914}). Brace believes that the consequent suspension of selection maintaining these features allowed mutations to accumulate which resulted in structural reduction. This explanatory mechanism is not widely regarded as valid, and to some extent its rejection has influenced acceptance of Brace's model.

Complete introduction
This is the opening paragraph to Wolpoff’s introduction. Read through to see how he begins with general ‘contextualising’  statements and how each sentence then contributes more precision leading to a more focused statement of the problem mentioned at the beginning.
The “Neandertal Question” – the place of the Neandertals in human evolution – is paleoanthropolgy’s  oldest problem (Spencer 1984), since the first archaic fossil to be discovered was a Neandertal. While it is also the material of the most recent headlines, the issues surrounding the place of the Neandertals in human evolution are better focused than ever before. The “Neandertal problem” is no longer perceived to be a worldwide one, although at least one solution proposed for it involves total population replacements on a worldwide scale. The Neandertals themselves, however, are recognised to be a geographic variant limited to Europe and western Asia, and it is widely accepted that the term “Neandertal” should not be used to describe the worldwide grade of hominids immediately preceding modern populations, if there was a definable grade (Wolpoff 1986). In particular then, the problem has become one of the relationship of Neandertals to the European populations of today.

Given the resurgence of interest in the behavioural changes associated with events surrounding the Middle to Upper Paleolithic transition in Europe, I believe that we must arrive at a solution to this problem, however tentative. Without a rather specific idea about the relation of the Neandertals to the populations that followed them, nothing else can be determined of the biological processes underway at the time of this archaeological transition. Adaptive models are only relevant if there was adaptive change. Models of physiological or reproductive advantages enhancing gene flow are only relevant if there was gene flow. The effects of behavioural changes cannot be analyzed if it was the compositions of the populations and not the evolutionary importance of their behaviours that changed. Without this basic knowledge, the causes of the transition, if there was a transition and not a simple replacement, cannot be understood. Thus, the phylogenetic relationships of these European populations are fundamental (Wiley 1981).

A rather different theory was set forth by Brose and Wolpoff (1971). We realized that what we regarded as transitional populations, such as Skhul and Qafzeh, seemed to follow the Neandertals in the Levant but were associated with the same local Mousterian industry, the Levallois-Mousterian. Therefore, we argued that the changes is selection resulting in these transitional Levantine populations must be based on adaptations that were part of the Mousterian culture, and we sought evidence for the direction of biological change in the Mousterian of central and western Europe, as Howells had. However, in contrast we believed that this direction was toward Upper Paleolithic European populations.

There is an alternative viewpoint about the origin of modern Europeans, perhaps most easily characterized by its extreme expression. This is the interpretation that Howells (1976) describes as the “Noah’s Ark” hypothesis and in a more recent expression has been dubbed the “Garden of Eden” theory (for instance as used in Stringer and Andrews 1988) – the proposal that modern groups have a single recent origin from a population that was already modern. Applied to the European situation this hypothesis means that modern Europeans did not originate in Europe. One of the main issues raised by this hypothesis is exactly where outside of Europe this place of origin was. A good deal of effort has been spent in seeking the earliest remains of the modern human form because it is reasoned that the earliest appearance of any modern population in the fossil record will be at the place of origin of all modern populations, a presumption which assumes the hypothesis to be tested, namely that modern populations have a single migrational origin. Over the years, the candidate for the earliest modern human has changed considerably, and therefore so has the place of origin. Cautiously, Howells has never been clear in his publications as to where this source area for the modern Europeans (and all other modern populations) might have been, but others have specifically suggested sub-Saharan Africa (Protsch 1975, 1978; Brauer 1984a, 1984b; Stringer 1984a, 1984b, 1985, 1986b, 1988; Stringer and Andrews 1988; Wainscoat et al. 1986a, Cannet al. 1987), the Levant (Vandersmeersch 1970, 1972, 1981a) or more generally western Asia (Howell 1951; Bodmer and Cavalli-Sforza 1976), China (Weckler 1957; Chang 1963; Mackintosh and Larnach 1976; Denaro et al. 1981), Australia (Gribbin and Cherfas 1982), or even more unexpected places (Hogan 1977). While sub-Saharan Africa has recently seemed the most favoured prospective human homeland, if the early dates for western Asia prove to be correct (Valladas et al. 1988) Israel may turn out to play an important role in both the biblical and scientific “Garden of Eden” interpretations of modern populational origins.
