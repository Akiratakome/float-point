Overview
The purpose of this learning object is to explore the principles underlying the writing of conclusions and the kind of language used in them. The conclusion to Paul Bruthiaux’s Predicting Challenges to English as a Global Language in the Twenty-First Century is provided as a model of a good academic conclusion. Key features are highlighted throughout.

Structure – This section considers the purpose of the conclusion in relation to the introduction and body of a paper, analyses its key components and the structure it should take in different forms of academic writing.

Strategy – Using a model conclusion taken from Bruthiax’s paper on language change this section analyses the 3 paragraphs the conclusion contains from the point of view of strategy. We see how the first paragraph restates the main points and reflects the structure of the paper. The second paragraph focuses on the most significant findings and provides perspective. The final paragraph emphasises the main findings and provides a clear ‘take-home’ message.

Language 1 – This section takes the 3 paragraphs of the conclusion and looks at their language in terms of discourse markers and collocations, tentative language and stylistic translation. There is a focus on differences between formal and informal style and guidance is provided on how to expand knowledge of key aspects of academic style.

Language 2 – This section provides guidance on using the Phrasebank Website which lists functional language used in conclusions.

Scientific conclusions – Using 4 conclusions from different areas of science this section analyses their similarities and differences.

 Shorter essays (2000 – 4000 words) in the humanities and social sciences

 Hide transcript

playmute
 The conclusion forms a critical part of an essay and students can fail to give it sufficient attention or time. The conclusion should offer a unified, logical ending, and has the purpose of bringing together the main ideas or findings of the essay and commenting finally on their significance. It should note any implications arising from the discussion of the topic, give recommendations if relevant and assess possibilities for further research. A conclusion then, looks back, looks deeply and then looks forward. Do keep in mind that this is your final statement on the topic, and that a strong conclusion will enhance the overall quality and impact of the essay or paper.

 Exam essays

 Hide transcript

playmute
 I’ll start out by comparing an exam essay to preparing a dinner. Probably not enough time is spent planning the dinner, leading to organisational problems later on. Too much time is then spent fussing around with the starter, or introduction, and while the main course may be full of material there are often too many potatoes and not enough meat. The real problem, however, comes at the end as there is very little time to prepare the dessert and a poor conclusion, too brief and too perfunctory, is just like serving up an over-ripe banana on a plate. A disappointment and something that actually detracts from the overall experience. It’s what the diners remember.
Examiners actually often go, when marking an exam script, to the conclusion first. What they hope to find is a clear summary of the main points or arguments related properly to each other. They hope to find a clear connection to what is set out as the thesis or aim of the essay given in the introduction. They hope to find a clear match with the development in the body of the essay and they want to see the student standing back and giving some final perspective on their arguments. So, having read the conclusion, the examiner is then properly prepared to go to the introduction and body of the essay and, crucially, is better able to judge the quality of the student’s work. In a nutshell, a bad conclusion devalues an essay and a good conclusion enhances it.

 Longer essays and dissertations in social sciences and humanities

playmute
 Your conclusion should refer back to your central topic as indicated in your title. It should also restate the key terms given in your introduction, along with a restatement of your hypothesis or thesis statement. This does not need to be done in a formulaic way but there does need to be a very clear match. A conclusion should have the aim of succinctly bringing together the key arguments and placing them in clear conjunction with each other. Note that for research-based papers a conclusion should also evaluate any research methods used, indicate any limitations of the research and outline future directions for research. A conclusion is not a mere restatement of the main points, however, as it has also the purpose of providing a sense of perspective by evaluating the significance of the study. The best way to learn is to see what good writers have done in the past. Look at some concluding sections from similar work in your own field and analyse the structure, strategies and tone of their conclusions.

I would agree with all that and would add a couple of points. First, on the difference between an abstract and a conclusion. An abstract is a brief synopsis of a completed piece of research, be it a dissertation, thesis or article . It appears at the beginning of the text but should be written after the conclusion has been made. An abstract summarises the introduction, purpose, methods, results and conclusion of the piece of research, so the terms of reference are broader. I have argued that geopolitical factors such as economic or military power are not sufficient to explain, let alone predict, the relative strength of languages of supranational communication. Clearly, critical mass is crucial and goes a long way toward making the current position of English as a language of global communication seemingly unassailable. It is also true that if this position is to be challenged in the twenty-first century, the most likely source of that challenge is likely to result – despite periodic bursts of Islamic assertiveness – from the rise of China as a world power in economic, military, and political terms. However, any challenge to the dominance of English as a global language will need to be based on more than a favorable set of geopolitical circumstances. In particular, I have suggested that complex inflectional morphology, tonal phonology, and a primarily logographic script would limit the chances of a challenging language to expand its global role. A global language is bound sooner or later to be used in large part by speakers who know it as a second language only and use it for a variety of purposes in a range of settings.  As it is pulled in different directions depending on degree of exposure to local languages and cultures in each setting, its linguistic characteristics are likely to be steered in the direction of greater inflectional simplification under pressure from instrumentally motivated users. This implies that to assume a global role, a language must be amenable to unplanned, user-driven adaptation, a process unlikely to succeed in settings with a strong tradition of centralized language planning and control, especially if power over language form and language use is concentrated in a single dominant culture.

Global potential and critical mass
A proposition running through writings on the worldwide spread of English (for example, Grabe 1988, Swales 1993) holds that languages of wider communication prosper or wither according to the amount of information they contain. Thus Sanskrit, Chinese, Greek, Arabic, Latin, French, and German all served—and in some cases, continue to serve—as depositories of privileged information—be it religious, legal, or technological—and as vehicles for the transmission and expansion of that information. Today, for a growing number of users, English functions as depository and transmitter of information related not only to science and technology but also, like its predecessors, to broader economic and cultural exchanges.

Clearly, English is not the only language currently fulfilling this kind of role. Following de Swaan (1998), languages can be seen as forming part of a global system consisting of several major constellations, each with its own set of local languages related to one central language, namely, the one spoken by the most multilinguals within the constellation. Some ten or twelve constellations can be readily identified. Some map fairly closely onto national borders and those of immediate neighbors (eg, Russian or German). Some, such as French or Portuguese, link an erstwhile colonial power and its former dependencies despite territorial separation, while others connect a dominant group in a large, socially complex country with other linguistic groups within the country and with a widely scattered diaspora (eg, Hindi or Chinese).

One of the less often mentioned languages operating in this fashion is Urdu, a language for which Aziz (1996) makes a plea, arguing that it should be taught in South Africa as a language of cross-cultural communication, to strengthen links between that country and the Indian sub-continent, and to foster mutual tolerance and understanding. Other languages of this type are Malay—in both its Bahasa Melayu and Bahasa Indonesia varieties—and Bengali, both spoken by more speakers than German or Japanese, yet rarely mentioned in this context, presumably because of the relatively minor geopolitical roles played by the nations with which they are associated. Within each constellation, the communicative potential of the dominant language is a factor of its prevalence as a mother tongue and its spread among multilinguals. This is an advantageous situation that the elites in charge of their respective sphere of influence are unlikely to relinquish, especially since maintaining this advantage becomes easier as nations grow wealthier.

The analysis proposed by de Swaan can be applied to the international reach of English in that the language now undeniably occupies the central position within this linguistic cosmology, increasingly connecting speakers of other dominant languages whenever communication is required across rather than within constellations. Thus, while English competes with Hindi in India and with Spanish and French in North America, it is now essentially without competitor at the global level, except perhaps in international organizations such as the United Nations or the European Union that  continue to reflect earlier geopolitical realities. By now, it is likely that the dominant position of English has become a self-reinforcing process. English is growing in supranational reach not only in direct relation to the amount of information it contains but also because the more people choose to learn it in one part of the world, the more attractive it becomes in other communities, exponentially. Thus, as de Swaan suggests, English may have entered the stage of a self-expanding global lingua franca, connecting all supranational languages in the global constellation in a network of communicative interdependence. At some point, de Swaan suggests, English might become the only second language being learned.

As suggested above, a key ingredient in the process of self-expansion is critical mass, the point at which sheer number of speakers, their wide geographic distribution, and their socio-political and economic power makes it too costly for a competitor to enter the fray. As in all monopolistic situations, lack of competition blunts incentives and inertia becomes a dominant characteristic of participants in the monopoly, be they providers or consumers. These circumstances probably already apply to speakers of English as their first language, who may see no benefit in making the effort to learn a second language. But it may increasingly apply to potential users of languages of international communication, who may see no reason to challenge the dominant global language since they typically have few emotional ties with that or any other supranational language. In her study of attitudes toward English among international graduate students on a US campus, Munro (1996) found that her subjects saw English as playing an essentially instrumental role in their plans. Subjects were somewhat passive in their acceptance of their future role as ambassadors for English and lacked incentives to support competitors to a language that had served them well and could be expected to underpin their career plans. To these homebound graduate students — the future policy shapers and predictors of cultural direction in increasingly outward-oriented nations — critical mass appears to have already ruled out any serious thought that there could even be a competitor for English as a global language.

Section from introduction outlining thesis of paper

Since no close precedent exists on which to base predictions, the linguistic seer is left to divine future trends from a combination of geopolitical trends and the sociolinguistic characteristics of languages with global potential. With this in mind, I consider the possibility that change may occur gradually and that a power may emerge later in this century to threaten American geopolitical supremacy and with it the dominance of English as a global language. I argue that even in the event of such a shift in geopolitical circumstances, the dominant language associated with that emerging power would first have to arrest and then reverse the advantage English now derives from critical mass. To do this, a global challenger would need to benefit from three key factors. Firstly, it would need to possess a set of linguistic characteristics that would facilitate its acquisition as a second language by individuals with largely instrumental motivations. Among these characteristics are minimal inflectional morphology, non-tonal phonology, and a non-logographic script. Secondly, to have a better chance of evolving such a set of favorable characteristics if it did not possess them already, a language with worldwide ambition would need to benefit from weak political and administrative control over form and usage and enjoy the freedom to accommodate unplanned, user-driven change leading to both structural simplification and a degree of creolization as the language adapts to local conditions in a multiplicity of sociolinguistic settings. Finally, a language competing for global prominence would have to be perceived—rightly or wrongly—as carrying the kind of sociocultural content and economic promise likely to fire the imagination of potential users and motivate them to undertake the task of learning the language, a challenge of some magnitude to most adult learners.

 It is important to recognize that while there are significant similarities between paragraph 2 of the conclusion and the statement of aims in the introduction the restatement of the aims comes in the light of the arguments made in the body of the article and this makes it easier for the reader to judge the success of those arguments.

 Paragraph 3: Narrowing to the principal thesis and the take home message.
The aim of the last paragraph is to narrow down to the principal thesis and main thrust of the paper and provide the take home message. Note how last sentence returns us to the title of the paper.

Taking a number of well-documented languages as examples, I have presented a review of some of the key characteristics that a hypothetical challenger to English as a global language would have to possess – or evolve – to have any chance of success. From this hypothetical perspective, all of the potential challengers discussed here – Arabic, Chinese, Esperanto, German, Japanese, Russian, and Spanish – exhibit several of these characteristics while only English exhibits them all, at present and for the foreseeable future. Meanwhile, current trends suggest that American power may undergo a degree of relative adjustment as a result of the growing importance of China as a world power. On the basis of the arguments presented in this discussion, it is predicted that even geopolitical alignment on this scale will not be sufficient to displace English as the dominant global language in the 21st century.

It is important to remember that different types of academic writing demand different approaches to conclusions. Here, for example there is no attempt to provide a critique of the methodology used because this is a review article. Similarly, no attempt is made here to suggest further directions for research. In other words the strategy used will be closely related to the nature and purpose of the writing.

Speaker 1
With regards to the interesting topic of scientific conclusions, well, it depends, on the format of the journal paper. Usually, you will have a "discussion" section, where you will put your main findings into the context of previous research and offer your own interpretation. This is a section where you can speculate, offering insights that are backed up by the data. In addition to the discussion, some journals also allow for a short last section, under the name of "conclusions".
This is really one or two paragraphs long (no more than that), and it is used to summarise the main finding and its relevance for the field. It serves to emphasise the key message of the paper (e.g. Neanderthals did interbreed with modern humans and this is important for so and so). Very often, actually, most often, the conclusion is merged into the discussion and constitutes the closing paragraph of the paper.
I think, in general, the same applies for a Masters or PhD thesis.

Speaker 2
I would agree with all that and would add a couple of points. First, on the difference between an abstract and a conclusion. An abstract is a brief synopsis of a completed piece of research, be it a dissertation, thesis or article . It appears at the beginning of the text but should be written after the conclusion has been made. An abstract summarises the introduction, purpose, methods, results and conclusion of the piece of research, so the terms of reference are broader. Secondly, I would add a small proviso concerning conclusions in theses or dissertations in the sciences. Personally, I like these to be longer than in a journal article and, while I recognize that many of the conclusions are embedded in the findings and discussion and the conclusions must be evidence-based, it is still a very good discipline for a budding scientist to be able to set out clearly and fully the main contribution of their research. This, of course, needs to be coupled with any comments on the limitations of the research and any future directions.

Discussion

Ceratocystis polychroma represents a new taxon that is consistently found in the larval tunnels of H. semivelutina on dying clove trees in Sulawesi. This fungus can also easily be isolated from the red-brown streaked living wood that is found associated with the borer. Morphologically, C. polychroma most closely resembles C. fimbriata. This explains why Liew et al.(2003) tentatively identified the fungus as that species. Both species have characteristic globose to oval ascomatal bases covered with hyphae, and hat-shaped ascospores accumulating in slimy masses at the apices of the ascomatal necks. Ceratocystis polychroma is morphologically different from C. albifundus (Wingfield et al. 1996) and C. pirilliformis (Barnes et al. 2003), the two other Ceratocystis spp. in the C. fimbriata complex. Thus C. albifundus is easily recognised by its pale coloured ascomatal bases and C. pirilliformis is different from C. polychroma in having distinctly pear-shaped ascomatal bases.

In conclusion, this study shows that physiological expression of “P301L” mutant murine tau is not sufficient to cause overt tau aggregation and toxicity during the normal lifespan of a mouse. Instead, this tau mutation causes changes, including alterations in mitochondrial transport, that are probably attributable to a loss of function or dysfunction of soluble tau. These changes could be some of the earliest and most decisive factors that precede, or precipitate the neurodegenerative process in tauopathies. It is possible that the transport changes observed here may be more wideranging than simply affecting mitochondria, in which case altered distribution of any number of cargoes could contribute to this process. However, mitochondrial dysfunction has been linked to pathogenesis in many neurodegenerative diseases (Lin and Beal, 2006; Morais and De Strooper, 2010), so a defect in mitochondrial flux could be critical. Of course, it will be important in the future to confirm that these transport changes also occur in cortical neuron axons, ideally in organotypic tissue once this becomes practical. Overall, the “P301L” tau knockin mouse not only sheds light on the relative contributions of tau mutation and tau overexpression to the development of tau pathology, but also advances our understanding of the normal and pathological functions of tau.

Microscopy and microanalysis of crystalline glazes Journal of Microscopy Pt 3, pp. 257-270

Conclusions

Using standard microstructural characterization techniques, crystalline glazes in the six artefacts examined in this work have all been shown to consist of macroscopic spherulites made up of a large number of acicular willemite crystals within a silica-rich glaze. The texture adopted by the willemite crystals within a spherulite, whereby the [001] directions are parallel to the axes of the acicular crystals, can be confirmed both by X-ray diffractometry and using transmitted light polarized light microscopy with a sensitive tint. While it is preferable for X-ray diffraction work that flat surfaces should be used as samples, slightly curved samples can also produce satisfactory diffraction data. X-ray microanalysis in the scanning electron microscope further confirms that the crystals within the spherulites are willemite. Partitioning of chemical species between the willemite spherulites and the glaze can also be measured conveniently by X-ray microanalysis. This was found to be consistent with expectations from practical experience of crystalline glazes reported in the ceramic art and craft literature.

Conclusions: A prediction for English as a global language
For your reference the complete conclusion to Bruthiaux’s paper is provided here.
I have argued that geopolitical factors such as economic or military power are not sufficient to explain, let alone predict, the relative strength of languages of supranational communication. Clearly, critical mass is crucial and goes a long way toward making the current position of English as a language of global communication seemingly unassailable. It is also true that if this position is to be challenged in the twenty-first century, the most likely source of that challenge is likely to result – despite periodic bursts of Islamic assertiveness – from the rise of China as a world power in economic, military, and political terms. However, any challenge to the dominance of English as a global language will need to be based on more than a favorable set of geopolitical circumstances. In particular, I have suggested that complex inflectional morphology, tonal phonology, and a primarily logographic script would limit the chances of a challenging language to expand its global role. A global language is bound sooner or later to be used in large part by speakers who know it as a second language only and use it for a variety of purposes in a range of settings. As it is pulled in different directions depending on degree of exposure to local languages and cultures in each setting, its linguistic characteristics are likely to be steered in the direction of greater inflectional simplification under pressure from instrumentally motivated users. This implies that to assume a global role, a language must be amenable to unplanned, user-driven adaptation, a process unlikely to succeed in settings with a strong tradition of centralized language planning and control, especially if power over language form and language use is concentrated in a single dominant culture.

To sum up, while increased geopolitical power is a factor that may well favor China – and hence Chinese – in the not too distant future, the critical mass accrued to English may well prevent radical change in current power relations between world languages. In addition, any hypothetical challenge to English as a global language would need to be facilitated by a cluster of linguistic factors including minimal inflectional morphology, non-tonal phonology, and a non-logographic script.

Were these key linguistic features not to be found in a language with nascent global ambition, adaptation in that direction under spontaneous pressure in a supranational context would have to meet with weak institutional resistance, a process that would be facilitated by a preference for skepticism towards the benefits of central language planning, which in turn would be aided by a multi-core geographic distribution among several self-confident cultural centers. Finally, a hypothetical challenger would have to be perceived as carrying a socio-cultural message capable of firing the imaginations of potential users and of motivating them throughout the learning effort.

Taking a number of well-documented languages as examples, I have presented a review of some of the key characteristics that a hypothetical challenger to English as a global language would have to possess – or evolve – to have any chance of success. From this hypothetical perspective, all of the potential challengers discussed here – Arabic, Chinese, Esperanto, German, Japanese, Russian, and Spanish – exhibit several of these characteristics while only English exhibits them all, at present and for the foreseeable future. Meanwhile, current trends suggest that American power may undergo a degree of relative adjustment as a result of the growing importance of China as a world power. On the basis of the arguments presented in this discussion, it is predicted that even geopolitical alignment on this scale will not be sufficient to displace English as the dominant global language in the 21st century.
