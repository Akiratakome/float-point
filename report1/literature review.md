Writing a Literature Review
Academic Development and Training for International Students: Writing a Literature Review
Introduction
This Learning Object is devoted to one of the most challenging sections of an extended piece of academic work: the literature review. The process of writing, revising and editing a literature review frequently involves more time and effort than any other section of a dissertation, report or thesis.  Here we aim to address some of the key questions concerning what a literature review is, what it should and should not contain and what characterises a particularly good one. We look at the strategies, the structure and the language and analyse these with the help of two very good models of literature review from different fields. Our overall aim is to provide you with a series of criteria to inform the planning of your literature review.

In Section 1 we attempt to come to an understanding of the general principles and functions of a literature review. Our aim is to prepare you for the analysis of model literature reviews in Section 2 and Section 3.

In Stage 1 we examine a range of definitions of a literature review and in so doing see that many factors have to be taken into account. Here we ask you to compare these with your own ideas and experience.

In Stage 2 a series of comments from supervisors are provided on issues and problems that characterise poor student written literature reviews. You are invited to reflect on these comments and the criteria underlying them.

In Stage 3 you can listen to four eminent Cambridge professors from different disciplines and who have extensive experience of working with postgraduate students giving their views on the characteristics of good literature reviews.

In Stage 4 we give some final reflections on other factors influencing approaches to writing a literature review.

In Section 2 we give an in-depth analysis of a literature review. We look at strategies, organisation and language. There are audio commentaries to guide you through each stage of the literature review.

In Section 3 we give an in-depth analysis of a longer, student-written literature review. This comes from a doctoral thesis and in Part 1 we look at how the author integrates a demonstration of her understanding of the literature with the strategic case she builds for her own research. In Part 2 we analyse the language of reporting and evaluating used in the literature review.

Section 4 is devoted to Expanding and improving your reporting language. Here we demonstrate three practical approaches to expanding the range of language you could use.

 Although this Learning Object can be viewed on a mobile phone or tablet, given the large amounts of text involved in analysing literature review, we advise users to view this on a computer screen.

 Stage 1 Defining a Literature Review
Defining what a literature review is in terms of its functions and purposes is not an easy thing to do. It depends much on the nature of the work you are doing and on the conventions of your discipline. It also depends, to some extent, on the expectations of individual supervisors. The amount of emphasis on aspects of what could be included in a literature review can vary significantly. Below is a characteristically bland reference to literature review from one department:


 The introduction should refer to relevant previously published papers and give the reasons for doing the work to be described. The emphasis is on setting the scene which existed at the start of the project so that the purpose of your research is made clear.


(Dept. of Plant Sciences, University of Cambridge)
This is all very true but it doesn’t really help a student plan what to focus on and it doesn’t help with understanding what makes one literature review better than another.  Transcript for interview with Professor Steve Young
Professor Steve Young is Professor of Information Engineering in the Department of Engineering at the University of Cambridge. He is currently Senior Pro-Vice Chancellor responsible for Planning and Resources.

 What are the purposes and functions of a literature review in an MPhil dissertation or PhD first year report or thesis?

Well, I should first of all say that I’m an engineer so I think my answers relate specifically to technology and science type research projects. I mean in those areas I think there’s three main purposes for the literature review. Firstly, you need to demonstrate that you’re aware of all the relevant existing work relevant to your research project. You certainly don’t want to get to the end of the thesis and be examined and for the examiner to say “Have you read X?” and you haven’t read X and X is rather critical to your work. Secondly, you need to demonstrate that you can assess the existing work critically. It’s not enough just to read a paper and tick that off the list, you have to understand it and when you reference a paper and if you want to say something about it then it should be clear that you really understand what the paper’s about. And then thirdly you need to use the literature review and your critical assessment of it to motivate your own research topic. You’re trying to set the scene as it were for the work that’s about to follow. So I think they’re the three main purposes of a literature review.

 What should be included?

Well, think of the literature review as a triangle. At the base of the triangle you need to sketch out the whole field. A good way to do that is to first of all pick out a few of the seminal papers that really started the field and then, if it’s at all possible, identify one or two good review papers which really bring you up to date but cover essentially most of the field. Then, on top of that, you need to focus on that part of the field which is directly relevant to your thesis to bring out the more recent work that probably exemplifies various different approaches to what you’re trying to do. And then, at the peak of the triangle, pick out the very most recent work. These are typically papers which have actually inspired the thesis itself. These are the ones that set out the ideas that inspire your work but also leave the gaps that you plan to fill in in your thesis.

 What are you looking for in particular in a good one?

Well, a good literature review should be interesting to read and be selective. It shouldn’t be just a simple long list of everything you read; it’s not necessarily the case that you’re going to talk about everything you read, indeed it’s unlikely that you would. So where there are multiple papers on a specific topic just pick out one which you regard as representative, succinctly describe its main contribution and then just simply refer to the others as a list. Use the literature to explain the principles. Again this is just not meant to be a dry description of every paper you’ve read. You should be drawing the pieces out of the papers that contribute to the field and when you bring them together they really fill out the picture of what the field’s about, why it’s interesting and why it’s relevant to your thesis.

 What faults do student written literature reviews tend to have?

Well I’ve probably already mentioned these. The main thing is to try to summarise too many papers and make the review into a long and tedious list of one paper after another without really bringing the papers together and explaining how they fit together as a whole. It’s not a recording of your diary, it really is meant to be a sampling of the literature which together brings out all the principle(s) in the field and specifically the ones that relate to your thesis.

 Do you have any particular advice for students?

Well, try and think of the literature review as telling a story. It’s sort of the story of the development of the field and also the story of how you came to the point where your thesis essentially starts. The literature review should really fill in everything that we’ve learnt so far about the field but there’s more to do. Try and keep the reader engaged and sufficiently interested in the problems you raise during the course of the literature review, that they’re actually eager to read the rest of the thesis and to see how you’ve extended the field of knowledge.

 Stage 4 Some Final Reflections
What have we learned so far? We see now that there are several key criteria that inform the planning and writing of a literature review. Students need to demonstrate an awareness and command of the underlying theory, of major contributions and recent studies in their field. They need to be able to analyse and assess the literature and present their review in a critical framework. Students need to use the literature review as a vehicle to situate their research and its key questions. In so doing the literature review provides a narrative of what motivates their research. Demonstrating the relevance of the key literature to a student’s own research is paramount. In addition, students need to think about whether to present their review chronologically or thematically, how selective they can be and where in their work to refer to the literature. These are strategic considerations. Students also need to be careful to present their work, with all its conventions of citation, in an acceptable manner and in a language that is appropriate, clear and concise.

All of this should inform the planning of a literature review. But, particularly for you as an international student, are there any other considerations? We would advise careful and detailed discussion of your literature review with your supervisor before starting it. A clear understanding of their expectations and the department’s guidelines are vital. We would also advise analysing and learning from good models in your specific field. This should be enough but sometimes students don’t know what questions to ask and sometimes they may be influenced by aspects of their own academic cultural traditions or even by other students who may not always offer the best advice.

Ettema, D. 2011. A multi-agent model of urban processes: Modelling relocation processes and price setting in housing markets, Computers, Environment and Urban Systems 35, 1–11

Paragraph 1
Land use transport interaction (LUTI) models have been used for several decades to evaluate the impact of transportation and land use policies. LUTI models describe the mutual interaction between land use, transport and accessibility. That is to say, they model travel behaviour as a function of the spatial distribution of functions over space, but also recognise that urban development is at least partly influenced by accessibility. A crucial element in such models is to model how population, defined by various segments, is distributed across space, since this distribution determines to a large extent which activity and travel patterns will evolve. The first generation of LUTI models (e.g. Lowry, 1963) were aggregate models (see Timmermans, 2003), describing the allocation of population as an aggregate category. Increasingly, however, LUTI models have become disaggregate models, culminating in agent-based micro-simulation models (e.g. Benenson, 1998; Ettema, de Jong, Timmermans, & Bakema, 2006; Miller, Hunt, Abraham, & Salvini, 2004; Moeckel, Wegener, & Schwarze, 2005; Waddell et al., 2003), which describe the location behaviour of individual households. These models not only adopt a representation of individual households, but also define individual dwellings as spatially fixed agents in the model. Given this level of detail, modelling the spatial allocation of population across space requires a proper representation of households’ decisions to locate into a certain dwelling and to switch from one dwelling to another. In other words, modelling housing markets becomes increasingly important in the context of agent-based LUTI models. It is important to note that housing prices play an important role in this respect. Competition for housing will increase prices, and may force households with smaller budgets to live In houses and in places which are not their first preference. Eventually, this will also impact on travel patterns, e.g. if they are forced to accept longer commute distances.

Nest position

Nest heights have been recorded for a number of chimpanzee populations (Table 3-10). It is at first unsurprising that nests are built higher in Issa than in Fongoli, as the trees are significantly taller in Issa. However, when the relative position of the nest within the tree crown is taken into account, chimpanzees in Issa are nesting higher even within the crowns of taller trees in Issa. Baldwin et al. [1981] found that chimpanzees in Assirik nested higher than chimpanzees in Equatorial Guinea, where trees were larger and proposed that nesting higher within the tree crown may be an anti-predator strategy (see Chapter 5). However, as Hernandez-Aguilar [2006] measured nests of all stages of decay, it is possible that differential decay rates of nests built at different heights within trees influenced this result. A detailed study of orangutan nest decay found that several factors including nest height and tree species influenced the rate of nest decay [Mathewson et al. 2008].

Similarly, the number of decisions was greater in Issa than in Fongoli and likely reflects the difference in tree size. This contrast suggests that chimpanzees in Issa are not nesting in the first available locations within trees, but instead higher and further out in the tree crown. This is congruent with the difference in the distance of the nest to the trunk and to the main stem between Fongoli and Issa. In order to compare nest building across populations of chimpanzees, one must control for differences in tree size and morphology.

 1: Analysis of selected extracts
Each of the 3 extracts in this section is chosen to illustrate an aspect of language use and its relation to strategy. There are different reasons to report and summarise and different ways to do so. Citation based reporting is where the only reference to the authors being cited is in the citation itself, there is no naming of them in the text. However, we often find the authors named in the text and the treatment of their work can be very direct or less direct. One question facing the writer of a literature review is which approach to use, when and why.

The aim of this section is to encourage you to read literature review with the eye of an author rather than that of a student. In addition to when to name the author in the text and when to cite, the extracts explore synthesising sources and literature review in the discussion section.

INTRODUCTION

All animals, vertebrate and invertebrate, spend proportions of their lives in a vulnerable state of sleep or sleep-like behaviour [reviewed in Lima et al. 2005]. Although the functions of sleep are still debated, researchers agree that the primary function of sleep involves neural maintenance [Siegel 2005]. However, sleep compromises the ability to detect predators [Anderson 1998; 2000; Lima and Rattenborg 2007; Lima et al. 2005; Rattenborg et al. 1999], and early research into sleep function highlighted the role predation pressure may have played in the evolution of sleep [Meddis 1975]. Some species have the ability to sleep only one half of the brain at a time, which enables the sleeper to detect predators [Rattenborg et al. 1999]. In an effort to address why more species do not engage in such unihemispheric sleep, Lima and Rattenborg [2007] developed a theoretical model that suggests complete behavioural shutdown (or unconsciousness) may be the safest way for animals to achieve necessary neural maintenance functions of sleep. Similar to trade-offs in predation risk versus energy intake in animal foraging strategies, there are likely trade-offs in predation risk versus sleeping site selection, sleep architecture, and sleep duration [Lima et al. 2005]. The evolution of shelter construction in the great apes may thus have been a solution to the trade-off between sleep and predation-risk, which allowed a large bodied ape to sleep recumbent in a safe, comfortable spot. REM (rapid eye movement) sleep in large mammals is accompanied by loss of muscle tone and so may require a stable platform and recumbent posture. Increased REM sleep has been proposed to have enabled cognitive evolution in great apes and Homo, through increased memory consolidation and enhancement [Coolidge and Wynn 2009; Fruth and Hohmann 1996]. (SCh5)

 Commentary
First let’s look at the degree of directness in reporting. All the verbs used here to some extent summarise an aspect of the study being referred to. This can be fairly direct or precise as in:

Kano [1979] classified nests into five categories

or

Rudicell et al. [in press] conservatively identified 67 different individuals.

Many of the verbs are used in more indirect summary such as:

Pruetz et al. [2008] addressed this issue in cross-site comparisons by contrasting nesting behaviour in Mt Assirik and predator-poor Fongoli in Senegal,…

or

McGrew [2004] framed three hypotheses of nest function in addition to that of recumbent sleep: anti-predation, antipathogen, and thermoregulation.

Clearly the choice of reporting verb is determined by what the author wants to report and the distance from the actual text they want to have. Sometimes you may want to report precise results and at other times what the authors did or a broad conclusion.

The second point to make is that some verbs are used much more often than others. This author favours ‘reported’ and ‘found’ and indeed these two words are often dominant in many literature reviews. The weakness of some student-written literature reviews is that the range of reporting verbs and sentence structures is too narrow and the review can read very repetitively. Here we see a very full range and it is advisable to scan through literature reviews in your own subject and create a checklist of as full a range as possible to select from when writing your own review. Look at the final section of this Learning Object for ideas on how to do this.

Finally, there is the importance of collocation. You see many collocations here highlighted in green. These are the words that are frequently found in combination with the reporting verbs. Some verbs carry a range of collocations such as ‘highlight’ and we see two here:

Wrangham [1987] highlighted the importance of the phylogenetic method for modelling behaviour of the LCA,… (SLR5)

Hernandez-Aguilar’s [2006] work highlighted the role of tree morphology in nest site selection by chimpanzees,… (SLR9)

The key when analysing a literature review from a language perspective is to notice these collocations as they are an important aspect of style and appropriateness.

We know from Section 1 that reporting via citation is often found in opening sections where the aim is to contextualise and provide prefatory remarks before going into more detail. This allows the author to refer economically to a range of studies on a broad theme. The example below comes from towards the end of the introduction to Chapter 5 and it is worthwhile noting that part of the aim in this paragraph is to demonstrate a lack of relevant previous study in the area being discussed.

Many studies have examined great ape nesting in detail and reveal preferences for particular species of trees, areas of the landscape, and morphological characteristics of trees [Ancrenaz et al. 2004; Brownlow et al. 2001; Fruth 1995; Hernandez-Aguilar 2006; Tutin et al. 1995; Wrogemann 1992]. A further reason to report via citation is to report a number of related studies but not ones which merit individual consideration, at least at this stage. The example below introduces 5 studies on a related theme.

Some studies have compared variables such as nest height, age, and tree species used, and have yielded more comprehensive knowledge of factors influencing nest material and location selection [Baldwin et al. 1981; Fruth and Hohmann 1994a; Fruth and Hohmann 1996; Hernandez-Aguilar 2006; Wrogemann 1992]. Some studies have compared …
Many studies have examined …
However, few studies have compared ….
No detailed comparison ………… has previously been done
(However,) evidence in support of … has been found in studies investigating …

A more sophisticated use of citation based reporting occurs when there is very indirect reporting. An analysis of the following two extracts shows that citation based reporting is ideal for integrating the briefest summary of a study’s conclusion within the narrative, as we see straightforwardly done in the first extract. In the second extract we find a neat way to combine conclusions, references and a key point the author herself is making.  In this second extract no reporting verbs are used at all.

Detailed analysis of nest-building techniques may indicate possible socio-cultural variation similar to some tool-using behaviours that are influenced by, but are not explained by, environmental conditions [Schöning et al. 2008]. (SCh6)
Thus, environmental influences on behaviour patterns do not preclude transmission of the behaviour through social learning [Laland and Janik 2006], and a correlation between genetic and behavioural similarities does not eliminate the likelihood that behaviours are culturally transmitted [Lycett et al. 2011]. (SCh3)

So, while the range of reporting verbs used in this kind of reporting and summarising is similar to that in direct reporting, the aims are different. Citation based reporting is most often used where the focus is on general reporting with an emphasis on inclusivity of representation before specific and particular studies most relevant to the area of research are introduced by naming the authors. As a rule citation based reporting reports the aims or scope of studies and sometimes the results of studies. It favours the maintenance of a clear narrative.
