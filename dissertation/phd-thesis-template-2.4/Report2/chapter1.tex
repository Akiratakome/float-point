\section{Reproducibility question}

High-resolution shock-capturing (HRSC) finite-volume solvers are increasingly
run on graphics processing units (GPUs), whose bandwidth suits data-intensive
stencil updates \citep{bard_dorelli_2014}. Bandwidth rather than arithmetic
limits such updates, and single precision (binary32, fp32) halves the bytes
moved per state relative to double precision (binary64, fp64). Production
astrophysics and CFD keep revisiting fp32 and mixed precision
\citep{brogi_etal_2024,wang_xia_chen_2025,higham_mary_2022}, yet the precision
is usually chosen once, for the whole code, and its effect seldom measured for
the scheme and problem in hand. Ideal magnetohydrodynamics (MHD) sharpens the
point beyond the Euler case, since its thin current sheets and near-degenerate
wave structure are places where a perturbation the size of a rounding error
need not stay that size.

Naming a second-order MUSCL--Hancock scheme with an HLL or HLLD Riemann solver
does not fix the precision, the compiler's floating-point transformations, the
branch taken at zero wave speed, or the processor type, so two implementations
of the published scheme may differ. What matters is whether the difference
stays below the discretisation or reference scale, or moves a resolved feature.
Reproducibility is used operationally here
\citep{plesser2018reproducibility,sandveEtAl2013reproducible}: IEEE formats
standardise operations and rounding, yet finite precision and reordered
reductions can still change a computed path
\citep{ieee754_2019,goldberg_1991,collange_etal_2015}. This report measures how
far they move a verified ideal-MHD HRSC implementation.

\section{How Report 1 informed Report 2}

Report~1 provided the controlled Euler baseline. It found no CPU/GPU difference
for matched strict-IEEE runs, but clear effects from fast-math compilation and
from the solver choice. Report~2 therefore keeps the device paths
matched, ports HLL to CUDA, and varies build semantics and the HLL/HLLD choice
deliberately. Section~\ref{sec:ch2-priorities} gives the full map, including
the planned move to ideal MHD.

\section{Research questions and scope}

The study asks four questions:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item\label{rq:mhd-validation} To what extent do numerical-reference
        comparisons and physical-property checks validate the central
        processing unit (CPU) ideal-MHD solvers and the tested graphics
        processing unit (GPU) Harten--Lax--van Leer (HLL) path?
  \item\label{rq:controlled-variation} For matched configurations, how do
        precision, compiler and floating-point options, Riemann solver and
        branch rule, processor type, requested thread count and the
        Courant--Friedrichs--Lewy (CFL) number alter the stored solution? What
        performance changes occur in repeated solver and CPU/GPU comparisons?
  \item\label{rq:scale-time-mca} How does the fp32--fp64 discrepancy vary with
        resolution and observation time, and how does it compare with the
        spread produced by Monte Carlo arithmetic, in which operations are
        randomly rounded at a chosen virtual precision?
  \item\label{rq:reproducibility-record} Which experimental, implementation,
        execution-environment and evidence records are needed to reproduce the
        tested ideal-MHD HRSC claims?
\end{enumerate}

The questions are answered for the Brio--Wu, Orszag--Tang and
Kelvin--Helmholtz ideal-MHD cases; Sod and Liska--Wendroff Configuration~3
provide a compact Euler continuity comparison.

\section{Contributions and report structure}

The report contributes an ideal-MHD extension of the Euler framework, with
generalised Lagrange multiplier (GLM) divergence control, CPU HLL and HLLD
paths, and a tested CUDA HLL implementation, together with a controlled
experiment pipeline that records effective build semantics and run metadata
from configuration through to plotting.
